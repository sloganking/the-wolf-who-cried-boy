# Where the Police Fit

---

Everything a container runs on is one kind of power: social authority. The agreements, [the promise](./before-you-facilitate.md#the-promise), the words you say when you ask someone to leave — they work because the people in the room agreed to be bound by them. That authority is real. It is also voluntary. It ends the moment someone stops caring about it.

[The walkthrough for threats of violence](./handling-threats-of-violence.md) ends its removal step with "you cannot be here." Everything after that assumes the person walks out the door. Sometimes they don't — and what happens then runs on a different kind of power, one that doesn't need anyone's agreement.

## When Someone Won't Leave

If someone who made threats refuses to leave, repeating the script louder doesn't change anything — the script's power was the agreements, and this person has stopped caring about them. If your event has security, they can escort the person off the property — and not just by asking. The bouncer walking someone out of a bar is real law, not a bar privilege: anywhere in the United States, once a person has been told to leave and refuses, whoever controls the property can use reasonable force to remove them. Reasonable means enough to get them out and nothing more — it can rise to match how hard they resist, it can never be deadly, and it ends the moment they're outside. And that is the whole of what security can do, because a guard has exactly the same legal powers everyone else has — the uniform adds nothing. So when getting someone out would take more force than *reasonable* covers, professional security calls the police — that's the security industry's own rule, because crossing that line makes the guard the one breaking the law. Security's sequence is words, then hands, then police. A facilitation team without security skips the middle: when words stop working, the next move is calling the police.

What exists for exactly this situation is a phone call: *"Someone is refusing to leave my property."* That's not reporting a crime. It's not an accusation, a case, or a verdict on anyone's character. It's a removal — what the law calls trespass: staying on a property after the person in control of it tells you to leave. The same call a restaurant makes at closing time. Once the person in control has said *leave*, staying is itself the offense. Police can remove the person without anyone having to decide what's true about anything that came before. And depending on your venue agreement — the contract or arrangement that got you the space — the person in control can be you as the event's host. What you say when you call shapes the encounter but doesn't determine it: officers who arrive for a removal and find evidence of a serious crime can act on it regardless of why they were called.

And above the removal call sits the one threshold that isn't a judgment call at all: someone moving toward violence, not just threatening it. That call is 911 — and [the promise](./before-you-facilitate.md#example-how-to-present-this) makes it ahead of time, before the container starts, so no one has to make it in the moment. Law-enforcement thresholds are the fire exits of a container: located before the event, hopefully never used, known cold. Decided in advance, they work the way the promise works everywhere else — [the decision was made before the fear showed up](./before-you-facilitate.md#when-the-facilitator-is-the-target). Left undecided, the question gets asked for the first time mid-crisis — by a facilitator who may be afraid, in front of a watching room, under exactly [the pressures that make fawning easiest](./before-you-facilitate.md#when-the-facilitator-fawns). And a container that will never call 911, no matter what happens, is showing the most dangerous person in the room that however far they push, words are the only thing that will ever push back.

You don't have to wait for a weapon, either. A threat can be [a crime all by itself](#the-local-law-checklist) — so a person still in the room, still making threats, is not an after-the-fact situation, and calling while it's happening is defense, not punishment. You also don't have to decide whether the threat legally counts before dialing: that assessment is the officers' job, not yours. The 911 call is only the clearest threshold — clear enough to decide in advance without knowing your container. Everything between a threat spoken and a weapon drawn is a judgment call — which is exactly why [it gets decided before the event too](#deciding-the-thresholds-before-the-event), not while the threats are still coming.

None of this contradicts the stance this book has held all along — it's the same stance, reaching a different severity. Mistakes get [repair, not punishment](./repair.md). Crimes get [a response that matches their severity](./appropriate-response.md) — and that response doesn't live inside the container.

## Why People Don't Call

Tell this story — death threats at a retreat, in front of witnesses — to someone who has never set foot in these spaces, and listen for the question that comes back: *why didn't anyone call the police?* Nobody in that room decided not to call. What happened is what a crisis looks like in a room running on stories instead of law — where what counts as a crime, what a call could help, and what it could hurt are all unknowns, while everyone's [filters](./trauma-and-filters.md#stories-control-attention) and [stories about what's right and wrong around sex](./body-stories.md#sex-is-politics) run at full strength. The same room that never thinks to call about a death threat can earnestly worry that an accidental boundary crossing might send someone to jail — [the imagined crime gets the fear, and the real one goes unreported](./when-youve-been-wronged.md#know-where-the-law-actually-stands).

From outside the story, the option is obvious. Inside the room, five separate mechanisms hide it:

- **The tool isn't filed.** People reach for the tools they're used to. Someone who has never called the police for anything doesn't have "call the police" filed in their head as an available action — and the [reticular activating system](./trauma-and-filters.md#try-this-right-now) already showed you what happens to options that aren't filed: they get filtered out before they reach the part of the brain that chooses. The idea to call isn't rejected. It never comes up.
- **The room's filters have already assigned the roles.** If the crowd has organized around the story that the person making threats is [protecting everyone](./why-rescuers-are-dangerous.md#rescuers-with-harmful-beliefs-the-righteous-predator), the roles are cast backwards: the righteous predator committing the room's only crime is wearing the hero's costume, and the person who committed no crime at all is wearing [the predator's costume](./trauma-and-filters.md#when-accusations-occur). Calling the police on the hero is unthinkable — and anyone who *is* thinking about the police is aiming at the wrong person.
- **The threatened person's own filter buries it.** The person being threatened has just been publicly attacked in front of a room that seems to agree. Their filter doesn't file the legal system as a remedy — it files it under *more people who will misunderstand and attack me*. The book has already shown where that leads: [terrified of a legal system that was pointed at the person threatening them the whole time](./when-youve-been-wronged.md#know-where-the-law-actually-stands).
- **The facilitators don't know the law either.** A facilitator who doesn't know the law doesn't know what a call brings toward a sex-positive event: protection, or scrutiny. That's not fear of being caught at something — nothing about the event is a crime. It's that officers are humans with [filters of their own](./trauma-and-filters.md), arriving mid-crisis at a scene [politics primes people to misread](./body-stories.md#sex-is-politics) — and an unpredictable system doesn't feel like protection. It feels like one more unknown danger. Which is the quiet argument under this whole chapter: [finding out where you stand before the event](#the-local-law-checklist) is what turns the legal system into a fire exit — located while the building is quiet.
- **The imagined machinery only comes in one size.** If the only legal frame anyone carries is the felony one — prison, a record, a life destroyed — then calling feels like firing the maximum weapon, and for anything short of the worst threat it registers as [over-response](./appropriate-response.md). What that frame misses is that the law is [graduated by severity](./severity.md#the-scale) too. At the bottom of the scale, having someone escorted off the property accuses no one of a crime at all. Above that, asking for a removal is a different call from reporting a crime — and reported threats have tiers of their own, from minor-offense versions up to the prison-and-record version everyone pictures. The machinery scales with what officers actually find. Believing it only comes in one size is what keeps it unused at every size.

## What Calling the Police Actually Starts

"Pressing charges" describes something that doesn't exist. On television, the threatened person decides: press charges and the case begins, drop them and it's over. In real life, everywhere in the United States, the decision to formally accuse someone of a crime — to file charges — belongs to a government lawyer called the prosecutor, who works for the government, not for either person involved. You don't file charges, and you can't drop them. The same fact protects the threatened person: nobody can pressure them to "drop the charges," because there's nothing in their hands to drop.

A call to the police starts a series of decisions, and every one after the first belongs to somebody else. The first is yours: whether to pick up the phone. Nobody comes asking — until somebody calls, none of the rest happens at all. Officers arrive and handle what's in front of them — the night ends with the person walked off the property, the person arrested, or a written report on file. Even an arrest only ends the night. It doesn't decide anyone's future: whether a case opens at all is the prosecutor's call, and guilt is a court's call after that, with each decision needing more proof than the one before. What opens a case is proof — and a threat leaves nothing physical behind. What proves it is the people who heard it, starting with the person it was aimed at. Anyone who heard it can say so, and one is enough — so a threatened person who stays silent weakens a case without necessarily ending it. What gets hard without them is the part only they can describe: fear is part of what makes a threat a crime, and with them absent it has to be shown by what people watched them do. And staying out of it isn't entirely theirs to decide either. A prosecutor who needs their testimony can get a court order requiring them to show up and testify — that order is called a subpoena. It isn't a request, and ignoring one carries its own penalties.

And none of it moves on its own. The law doesn't require police to investigate any particular report, and prosecutors put their limited hours on the most serious cases first — some offices close minor cases without ever looking at them unless the person who reported calls back to push. So television's other story — *report it and the government takes it from here* — is as fictional as "pressing charges." For anything less than the most serious threats, a report nobody pushes can sit in a file forever. That's not the system failing. It's the system doing what this book teaches — [matching the response to the severity](./appropriate-response.md) — with the whole city's crimes competing for the same hours. And it's one more reason the threatened person's participation carries so much weight. A threat can be charged either way — the serious tier or the lesser one — and which one it becomes is the prosecutor's choice, not a fact about what happened. The further down it lands, the less it moves without somebody who keeps telling the story and keeps asking for action.

Hold that picture — something you start but don't steer, that moves only as far as proof and pushing carry it — up against the two opposite fears that keep phones in pockets:

- *"If I call, their life is over."* Calling is not convicting. Between the call and any consequence stand an officer's assessment, a prosecutor's judgment, and a court that requires proof — the same verification the mob skipped, [required by law this time](./when-youve-been-wronged.md#know-where-the-law-actually-stands).
- *"I can call now and steer it later."* You're starting a process, not steering one — the only moment you fully control is the moment before you dial. Which is exactly why the call deserves what this book asks of [every response](./severity.md#your-response-has-a-severity): know its severity before you choose it.

For imminent danger, the call is defense — and defensive actions are always acceptable. After the fact, it's a choice — and not only the threatened person's. Anyone who witnessed a crime can report it: a facilitator, a staff member, a participant who watched it happen from across the room. No one needs permission to report what they saw, and their reasons can be their own. What none of them gets is the threatened person's decision — filing that person's account for them, or pushing them to take part, spends something that costs them and not you. That choice stays theirs, informed and unpressured.

## Deciding the Thresholds Before the Event

The trade-off deserves naming plainly: a police car arriving at a sex-positive event is itself a container event. There's privacy and outing risk. There are attendees with rational reasons to fear police contact. There's the venue relationship, and in some places, questions about whether the event itself is on solid legal ground. Every one of those costs is real — and weighing them for the first time in the middle of a crisis is how the weighing never happens. That's the case for settling the thresholds before the event. What the room needs to hear is short, and it belongs in the same breath as the promise:

> "If anyone is in imminent physical danger, we call 911. That was decided before tonight, and it will not be debated in the moment. Threats of violence may also be a police matter — one we will not shield you from."

That's the whole public version. A facilitator might read that second sentence and see fear being imported into a room that came for something else — thinking that a group gathered for connection doesn't need police in its opening breath. It works the other way. A safety briefing naming interference with a flight crew as a federal offense doesn't put a hijacking in anyone's head; it names a behavior, and a named behavior only sounds like a threat to someone weighing it. That's who it's for. Someone who believes that standing up for a person in the room can't cost them more than a raised voice is missing the fact that would change what they do next — and the only moment they can still use it is before anything has happened.

Everything else is preparation, and it sits behind the promise as three facilitator duties:

1. **Document.** [While it's fresh](./when-youve-been-wronged.md#document-what-actually-happened), including who was present — a threat made in front of a room full of witnesses is provable in a way a private one isn't.
2. **Inform — everyone, including yourself.** Nobody can decide this with a head full of television law. Give the threatened person the real picture in plain words: *"come get this person off the property"* and *"I want to report a crime"* are two different phone calls — the first one doesn't start a case, it just ends the night. If they report, the police investigate, and a prosecutor — not the caller, not the officers — decides whether anyone gets charged. And making the call convicts no one: nothing about picking up the phone decides how far it goes. And time is more forgiving than it feels. In California, a threat to kill or seriously injure someone can be reported for three years — and it stays three years even when it ends up charged at the lesser tier, because the clock is set by the most the law could do about that crime, not by what the prosecutor picks. Threats that don't reach that description — a vague one nobody could tie to a specific harm, a shouting match that frightened the room, repeated harassing calls or messages — are lesser offenses, and generally carry one year instead. What doesn't last three years is the evidence — witnesses scatter, memories blur, and tonight's account is worth more than the same account in six months. They don't have to decide right now. It just gets lighter the longer it waits. Then tell them the part that's about you. If there's something they could tell you that would make you call regardless of what they want, say what it is before they start talking. Otherwise they're choosing what to disclose without knowing which words take the choice out of their hands.
3. **Support the threatened person, without steering their decision.** Pushing them to report is [rescuing](./why-helping-is-hard.md#dont-be-a-rescuer) — taking over a decision that belongs to them. Pushing them *not* to report, to protect the event or the community's reputation, is [protection going everywhere but to the threatened person](./before-you-facilitate.md#when-fear-decides-who-gets-protected). The support itself is concrete. Tell them they don't have to decide tonight. If they do call, or if officers want to talk to them, they don't have to do it alone — someone goes with them. Keep them posted on what you're doing about the person who threatened them, so they aren't left guessing whether anything happened. And whatever they land on, nothing about how you treat them changes — which is the only thing that makes it a real choice instead of a test they're trying to pass.

Those three duties are all about the threatened person. Your own part is a separate question, and it starts with the two accounts coming apart: what you report is what you saw, and you don't need anyone's permission to report it. Their account is theirs — you don't have it, and you can't file it for them.

What that leaves is the one line you'll actually want drawn. Somewhere between a shouted threat and someone bleeding on the floor is the point where you stop asking the threatened person what they want and call anyway — and it's worth locating while nothing is happening, because it costs in both directions. Call over the threatened person's objection and you may hand them the exposure they were trying to avoid, plus a court order to come describe the worst night of their life. Stay quiet because they asked you not to, and one person's preference has set the terms for everyone else in the room, and everyone in the next one. Deciding early doesn't make either cost go away. It means you pick while you can still think — and the threatened person, when they decide how much to tell you, knows what they're deciding into.

Two questions come with the call itself: who makes it, and what happens while you wait.

Anyone in the room can make it, without asking you or your staff. Nothing about your job changes: the threatened person still goes out first, the person who made threats still leaves after them, and the officers who show up get an accurate account of what happened.

While you wait, you may be asking yourself whether you should try to keep the person who made threats there until officers arrive. Don't. The law does allow what people call a citizen's arrest — a private person may arrest someone for an offense committed in front of them — and that permission is what makes it a trap. It's only lawful if an offense actually happened, and whether a threat clears that bar is the assessment this chapter already leaves to the officers, because it turns on things nobody can settle mid-room: whether the threat was specific enough, and whether it left the person genuinely afraid. Guess wrong and the people who did the holding are the ones facing charges — false imprisonment, or worse. Holding someone longer than the moment calls for is its own offense even when the arrest was sound.

The physical arithmetic is worse than the legal one: holding someone turns a person who was leaving into a person who is fighting, in a room full of people you're responsible for. A container's authority is to make someone leave, not to keep them. And if they're gone by the time officers arrive, the report still works — that's what the witnesses and the documentation are for.

## The Local-Law Checklist

None of these questions has one answer everywhere. Threatening someone is a crime under a different name two states over. Recording a conversation needs one person's consent in some states and everyone's in California. In New Jersey and Wyoming every adult is legally required to report suspected child abuse; in most states that duty falls on listed professions only. Across national borders the spread is wider again.

Before you host — especially somewhere new — find these answers the way you'd locate the fire exits:

1. **What threatening someone is called** where you're hosting — criminal threats, terroristic threats, menacing, intimidation — and which tiers it can be charged at. Some places make it one or the other; some leave the choice to the prosecutor, so the same words become a felony or a misdemeanor depending on a decision made later.
2. **The emergency and non-emergency numbers** for the venue's location, and how long a response actually takes there. A rural retreat is not a city hotel.
3. **What it takes to have someone removed, and who holds the right to ask.** If you don't own the venue, your venue agreement decides whether that person is you or the owner. If it's the owner, settle in advance how a removal happens when they aren't on site — nobody should be hunting for a landlord's number with a crisis in the room. This is only about the removal call: reporting a crime, or calling 911 about danger, takes no property rights at all.
4. **If you hire security — what they handle themselves, and when they call the police.** They work under the same law you do, and the uniform adds no legal powers. Where their line sits is a judgment call, so draw it in the contract rather than in the hallway.
5. **Mandatory-arrest rules** — about sixteen states and DC require officers to arrest whenever they find probable cause of domestic violence, and roughly ten more require it under specific conditions like a visible injury or a violated protective order. Where those rules apply, the decision is out of the officers' hands and yours.
6. **Protective and restraining orders** — the court orders that require someone to stay away from a person. Which emergency versions exist where you are, and how fast they actually move.
7. **Who decides whether anyone gets charged.** Everywhere in the United States it's a government lawyer, the prosecutor — nobody else files an accusation, and nobody can withdraw one. Worth confirming separately if you host outside the US.
8. **Whether anyone on your team is legally required to report.** There's no general duty to report a crime you witnessed; staying quiet isn't itself a crime. But every state names roles that *must* report suspected abuse of minors, elders, or adults with physical or mental limitations — commonly licensed therapists and counselors, health practitioners, social workers, clergy, and anyone in a caretaking role. New Jersey and Wyoming extend the duty to every adult where child abuse is suspected. The trigger is reasonable suspicion rather than proof, and it holds whether or not the affected person wants a report made. It usually attaches to what someone learns *in their professional capacity or on the job* — which leaves a question only your container can settle: is a therapist working your event acting in their professional capacity? Many institutions instruct their people to report either way, and the law protects them when they do. These spaces are full of therapists and bodyworkers; if one is on your team, find out together where their obligation starts.
9. **Recording-consent law** — documentation can include recordings, and the rules split: some states need one person's consent, others need everyone's. California requires all parties for conversations where people expect privacy.
10. **For international events:** the local law on threats, how local police relate to an event like yours, and whether an event like yours is legal there at all. All three vary more than anything else on this list.

One person can go find all ten, and [your conflict specialist](./before-you-facilitate.md#your-conflict-specialist) is the obvious candidate — they're the one who'll be using the answers. When something happens, they run it and you back them: a division agreed before the container, not negotiated inside it. If nobody on your team holds that role yet, the answers are yours. Offered as a recommendation and nothing stronger — the doors don't open until the list is done.

None of this makes you a lawyer, and none of it is legal advice. It's the same move as [knowing where the law actually stands](./when-youve-been-wronged.md#know-where-the-law-actually-stands) before fear answers for you — applied to the container instead of yourself. Find out where the exits are while the building is quiet.
