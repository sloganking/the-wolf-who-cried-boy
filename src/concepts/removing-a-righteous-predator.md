# Removing a Righteous Predator Without Taking on Personal Risk

---

[Why Rescuers Are Dangerous](./why-rescuers-are-dangerous.md) names the pattern. [Handling Threats of Violence](./handling-threats-of-violence.md) covers the moment a threat is live in the room. Neither one answers the question an organizer actually loses sleep over: *how do I get this person out of my community without it costing me my community?*

That's the gap this page fills. Not morality — you already believe removing them is right, or you wouldn't be reading this. What you don't have is a way to do it that doesn't end with your own reputation, your event, or a year of your life on the line instead of theirs.

Everything below is written from your own interest. Not because your interest is more important than doing the right thing — because a structure built on your safety is one you'll actually use under pressure, and a structure that asks you to be brave first is one you won't.

There's a word for what you're building, and [All Power Is Mutual](./all-power-is-mutual.md#top-safe-bottom-safe-and-staff-safe) names it alongside top-safe and bottom-safe: **staff-safe** — can the person the rules require to act do it without taking on personal risk of their own? This page is the staff-safe list, worked all the way through for the hardest case a container has: a contested removal.

## Why It's Harder

A selfish predator hands you something to point to: a mistake, a pattern, a rule broken in a way witnesses can describe. Removing them is a clean call, because the record does the arguing for you.

A righteous predator doesn't hand you anything. Their weapon is the accusation itself. The moment they name someone a threat, removing *them* — the accuser — looks, to a room that hasn't verified anything yet, like siding with the person they've already convicted. You're not choosing between "act" and "don't act." You're choosing between two actions that both look, from outside, like you picked a side before you knew anything. And the room is watching you choose in real time, with [certainty already running](./why-rescuers-are-dangerous.md#the-five-dangers-of-certainty) on one side of the room and nothing yet on the other.

The timing makes it worse. A selfish predator's harm shows up while it's happening — there's a moment you can point to. A righteous predator's harm lands after: the story that outlives the event, the reputation that doesn't come back when everyone goes home, the community members who [absorbed the narrative](./before-you-facilitate.md#why-facilitator-silence-is-different) because nobody with more authority said otherwise. By the time you can see the full cost of not acting cleanly, the room that would have helped you act has already dispersed.

This is why "just be brave" doesn't work here, and why this page doesn't ask you to be. [The Promise](./before-you-facilitate.md#the-promise) already runs on the same insight: decide before the fear shows up, so the moment doesn't require courage — it requires following a decision you already made. Everything below extends that same move to the hardest case the Promise has: a contested removal, with a room watching, and a person telling you that hesitating makes you complicit.

## Remove for Behavior, Never for Identity

"Righteous predator" is a word for your own head. It explains a pattern to you so you can see it coming. It is never a word you say in the room, to them, to your staff, or in any statement to your community — not because it's unkind, but because [labels end verification](./why-rescuers-are-dangerous.md#case-study-circumcision) the moment they're applied, and you need verification to stay open until the outside panel below has actually looked.

What you name instead is behavior — and here's the self-interested part: **you almost certainly already have the grounds.** A righteous predator, doing what righteous predators do, tends to walk straight into agreements your container already has. [The Promise's](./before-you-facilitate.md#suggested-guidelines) existing lines cover most of it without any new rule:

- Making threats or intimidating someone into silence
- Demanding someone's removal with no process — pressuring you to convict on their say-so
- [Destroying someone's reputation](./before-you-facilitate.md#suggested-guidelines) — organizing others, spreading the accusation before anyone's checked it
- [Using victim status — their own, or someone else's — as license to attack](./power-dynamics.md) rather than to report and let the process run

Every one of those is already a violation of the same agreement they consented to at the door. You are not inventing a new standard to catch them. You're recognizing that the person demanding an off-the-books removal is, in the act of demanding it, breaking the same rule they're invoking against someone else. That symmetry is what makes this defensible instead of personal: you're not deciding you dislike them. You're enforcing the identical standard on the person applying pressure that you'd apply to the person they're pressuring.

## Before the Crisis: The Agreement, Extended

This book has already built curiosity before verdict — you just haven't had to name it as its own line yet. [Are You a Qualified Source?](./before-you-judge.md#are-you-a-qualified-source) and the [PAUSE → VERIFIED?](./appropriate-response.md#response-flowchart) flow are the practice. What's missing isn't the idea. It's a sentence participants agree to before anything happens, so that when it does, you're not introducing a new standard mid-crisis — you're pointing at one they already accepted.

Add two lines to [the Promise](./before-you-facilitate.md#the-promise), read aloud with everything else:

> **If something happens, we get curious before we get certain.** A report is information, not a verdict. We will talk to the people involved before we decide anything — and separating people to keep everyone safe, if that becomes necessary, is safety, not a verdict about who was right.
>
> **If a removal is contested — if the person being asked to leave disputes what happened, or the accusation itself is what's in question — that decision does not stay with me.** It goes to people outside this container, agreed on in advance, whose only job is to look at what happened and decide. I commit, in writing, right now, to whatever they decide.

Say both before you need either. The second line is the one doing the real work for you: it tells the room, in advance, that a contested case was never going to be decided by whoever was loudest, or by you under pressure — so when the moment comes, you're not making that decision. You're honoring one you already made.

## During: A Named Team, Not a Verdict

Everything [Your Conflict Specialist](./before-you-facilitate.md#your-conflict-specialist) and [your first officer](./walking-your-talk.md#your-first-officer) already do applies here at full strength — a named team exists precisely so no single person owns a call the mob can pin on them. If threats of violence are part of what's happening, [the order of operations](./handling-threats-of-violence.md#the-order-of-operations) runs exactly as written: safety first, and [separation is not a verdict](./when-things-go-wrong.md#the-emergency-exception) — it's what buys you the time to get the actual decision right.

What this page adds to the threats walkthrough is the case with no physical threat in it at all: pressure. Cornering you, recruiting other participants, narrating the incident to anyone who'll listen before you've said a word. Handle it the same way you'd handle a threat you can't yet verify — contain it, don't debate it in the moment:

> "I hear that you're certain, and I'm taking what you're telling me seriously enough to look into it properly. I'm not deciding anything about anyone's standing here tonight, and I'm not the one who decides a contested case — that's not a new rule, it's the one we all agreed to at the start. Here's what happens next: [the report goes to the team / the outside panel, on this timeline]. I need you to let the process run instead of running it yourself."

Say it once, calmly, and repeat it verbatim if pressed. It isn't a debate performance — the sentence's job is to be boring enough that a room watching for a fight doesn't get one. A leader who won't be drawn into arguing the merits in real time is showing the room a process is running, not a personality contest.

## After: The Outside Panel

This is the piece that actually removes the personal risk, because it moves the verdict off of you.

**Commit to it before any case exists — in writing, in the same document as the Promise.** Contested removals — cases where the accused disputes what happened, or where the accusation is the disputed thing — go to people outside your organization: not your staff, not your community, people known elsewhere for handling exactly this. You follow what they decide. This isn't a new invention. It's the same structural move behind the [US Center for SafeSport](https://uscenterforsafesport.org/about/faqs/), which investigates abuse and misconduct across Olympic and Paralympic sport independently of the sports federations themselves — federal law bars those federations from interfering in or trying to influence the outcome of a Center investigation. It's the same logic behind aviation's separation of who hears a safety report from who can discipline a pilot: [NASA collects the reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_Safety_Reporting_System), and NASA has no power to ground anyone, which is exactly why pilots trust the channel enough to use it. And it's the same reason workplaces and universities retain outside investigators and ombuds instead of asking a department to judge its own member — the neutrality has to be structural, not a matter of someone's personal restraint, or it doesn't survive the first hard case.

**What it buys you is specific.** When the panel rules, you get to say — and mean — *"I'm not the expert on this. We hired the experts. This is what they decided, and this is what we're doing."* People can question the panel. They can argue with the panel. What they can no longer do is make the removal about whether you, personally, are corrupt, cowardly, or complicit — because the verdict isn't yours. You didn't judge your own community member. You enforced an outside decision you committed to before you knew who it would ever apply to.

**What it needs to actually hold:**

1. **Selection happens outside the heat of any case** — ideally people your community already has some reason to respect, named in your written agreements if you can manage it, so "rigged" has nothing to land on.
2. **It never replaces the emergency move.** A live threat still gets [separated tonight](./handling-threats-of-violence.md#the-order-of-operations). The panel decides the verdict after safety is handled, not instead of it.
3. **Scale the cost to your event.** A small container doesn't need to build its own panel from scratch — several organizers can share one, the way small clinics share a review board instead of each running their own.

**After the panel decides, two more things protect you, and neither one is optional.**

*A short, neutral statement to the same audience the accusation reached.* If the room heard the accusation, the room hears the resolution — not a verdict on anyone's character, just what happened procedurally: "A contested report was reviewed by our outside panel, per our published agreements. [What was decided.] That process is complete." This is the same move [handling-threats-of-violence.md](./handling-threats-of-violence.md#what-to-say-to-the-crowd) already teaches for the violent case, extended to the non-violent one: [the room fills a silence with a story](./before-you-facilitate.md#when-its-been-months) whether you provide one or not, and an uncorrected story doesn't expire on its own.

*Documentation, contemporaneous and specific.* What was reported, what the panel reviewed, what it decided, and when — written down while it's fresh, the same discipline [the emergency exception](./when-things-go-wrong.md#how-to-handle-it) already asks of you for a safety call. You're not building a case against anyone. You're building the record that shows, later, that a process ran — which is the single most protective thing you can have if the removal is ever challenged.

## The Retaliation Plan

Decide this before you need it, because a righteous predator who's removed doesn't necessarily stop. They may post about it, call people, try to recruit others to their account of what happened. Plan for it the way you plan for the emergency itself — in writing, in advance, so nothing about your response has to be improvised by someone who's already exhausted from the case itself.

**The policy speaks. You don't.** Every response to every post, call, or message about the case is the same short, boring line, said by whoever's designated to say it — not you personally, if you can help it, and never a different answer each time:

> "This was reviewed and decided through our published process. We stand behind that process and won't be discussing the specifics further."

**No one-on-one fights.** Not you, not your staff, not sympathetic community members drawn into defending the decision in the comments of someone else's post. Every individual engagement is a new front, and a righteous predator running on certainty [treats resistance as proof they're right](./why-rescuers-are-dangerous.md#the-five-dangers-of-certainty) — arguing back doesn't de-escalate this pattern, it feeds it. The consistency of the response is what makes it boring, and boring is what makes it stop.

**Your restraint here is also your legal ground**, and it's worth knowing why. A good-faith, factual statement — made to people who have a legitimate reason to hear it, and limited to what you can actually document — is the kind of statement defamation law protects. A statement made in anger, personalized, or reaching further than your actual audience is not. The policy-speaks script above isn't just calmer. It's the version that holds up.

## The Lawyer Test

Here's the case for all of this that has nothing to do with what's right, and everything to do with what protects you.

The law rewards exactly the shape this page has been building: a written policy, applied consistently, to documented behavior, decided by a process instead of a person. That's not this book's opinion — it's the same shape courts look for when they ask whether an organization handled a complaint responsibly, and it's the same shape that protects a good-faith statement from becoming a defamation claim: made to the right audience, limited to what you can back up, free of personal animus. What exposes you is the opposite of all of it — enforcement that looks arbitrary, a decision that tracks who you personally liked, a statement that reached further or hit harder than the facts did.

**Put plainly: the very same structure that gets a righteous predator out of your event cleanly is the structure that protects you if any of it is ever challenged afterward.** You're not choosing between doing the safe thing and doing the right thing. The outside panel, the written commitment, the documentation, the boring consistent response — that's the version a lawyer looks at and says *do that.* Not because it's generous. Because it's defensible, and defensible is what actually protects you, your staff, and everyone still coming to your events after this one.

**The organizer who has to be brave in the moment has already lost the position.** The organizer who decided in advance never has to be.

## The One-Page Checklist

**Before any case exists:**
- [ ] Written agreement includes curiosity-before-verdict and "contested removals go to an outside panel," read aloud with the rest of the Promise
- [ ] Outside panel identified — named if possible, shared with other organizers if your event is small
- [ ] Your team knows who's designated to speak publicly if retaliation happens, and it usually isn't you

**When it starts:**
- [ ] Never say "righteous predator," or any character label, in the room — name the behavior that broke the agreement
- [ ] Live threat of violence → [handling-threats-of-violence.md's order of operations](./handling-threats-of-violence.md#the-order-of-operations), immediately
- [ ] Pressure without a threat → the containment script above; the verdict does not get made in that conversation
- [ ] A named team handles it — never one person alone

**After a contested case:**
- [ ] The outside panel decides — not you
- [ ] A short, neutral, factual statement goes to the same audience the accusation reached
- [ ] What happened, what was decided, and when — documented while it's fresh

**If retaliation follows:**
- [ ] One scripted response, repeated verbatim, from one designated person
- [ ] No one-on-one arguing, by you or by anyone on your behalf
- [ ] Every statement stays factual, limited to your actual audience, free of personal characterization
