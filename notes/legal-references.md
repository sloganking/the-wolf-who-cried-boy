## Q: Can anyone report a crime, or does the victim have to be the one who reports?

**A: Anyone can report a crime — victims, witnesses, or third parties with information.** No property rights, no victim status, no relationship to the event required. Whether police *investigate* and whether a prosecutor *charges* are separate downstream decisions (in a threats case the victim's account is often most of the evidence, so a victim who won't participate can end a case in practice — but the report itself is open to anyone). Some jurisdictions even formalize third-party reporting for sensitive crimes (e.g., Illinois 725 ILCS 203/22 for sexual assault; UK third-party reporting centres).

## Q: Can security physically remove someone, or can they only ask? (The bouncer question)

**A: Physical removal is legal, within limits — and the limits are general American law, not a special bouncer power.** The doctrine (common law, adopted broadly across states): the owner or lawful occupant of a property may use **reasonable, non-deadly force** to eject a trespasser, after the person has been asked to leave and refused within a reasonable time. Force may scale in proportion to the trespasser's resistance and the threat they pose, must stop when they're out (or comply), and may never be deadly just to remove someone — deadly force requires a separate self-defense justification (imminent death / great bodily injury). So the TV bouncer physically walking someone out is real law; the TV bouncer beating someone in the alley is battery.

**California version:** CALCRIM No. 3475 ("Right to Eject Trespasser From Real Property") — owner/lawful occupant may use reasonable force after a refused request to leave, if a reasonable person would see the trespasser as a threat to the property or occupants; force may increase in proportion to resistance.

**Industry practice** matches the legal shape: professional security standards are de-escalate, give clear notice and an exit path, keep any physical contact minimal (open-hand guidance, escort positioning), and **call police early when refusal continues** — because force past "reasonable" makes the guard the one committing a crime, and "reasonable" is decided later by a jury looking at the facts.

## Q: Do security guards have legal powers a regular person doesn't?

**A: No. A security guard is the property owner's agent, with the same legal powers as any other private person.** The uniform and badge add zero legal authority — California's own state training manual for guards (BSIS "Powers to Arrest") says exactly this: "A security employee has the same power to arrest as any other private person." What a guard exercises is the *owner's* rights (ask people their business, ask them to leave, use reasonable force on a refusing trespasser — see previous question) plus the citizen's-arrest power everyone has (in CA, Penal Code § 837: offense committed in your presence, or felony grounds). A citizen's arrest must be handed to police without delay, and impersonating a police officer is itself a crime. Practical upshot for an event: hiring security buys trained hands and a calmer ladder, not extra law — their ladder tops out at the same place yours does: the police.

## Q: Is California criminal threats (Penal Code 422) really chargeable as a felony?

**A: Yes — PC 422 is a "wobbler":** the prosecutor may charge it as a misdemeanor (up to 1 year county jail, $1,000 fine) or a felony (16 months / 2 / 3 years state prison, $10,000 fine), based on the circumstances and the person's history. A felony conviction is also a strike under California's Three Strikes law. The book's phrasing ("can be charged as a felony carrying prison time") is accurate — "can be," not "is."

- Sources: [Shouse Law — PC 422](https://www.shouselaw.com/ca/defense/penal-code/422/); [WK Law — wobbler analysis](https://www.wklaw.com/criminal-threats-misdemeanor-felony-california-pc-422/); statute text of PC 422(a)

## Q: Does criminal law really require intent — is an accidental boundary crossing typically not a crime?

**A: Yes, with the mechanism worth knowing.** Crimes require a guilty state of mind (*mens rea*). Battery requires a *willful* touching — an accidental, involuntary touch is not battery even if it causes harm (e.g., CALCRIM 960: "willfully... in a harmful or offensive manner"; California defense sources state directly: "If the contact was not willful, then it is not battery even if injury resulted"). Sexual battery (CA PC 243.4) goes further — it's a *specific intent* crime: the touching must be done *for the purpose of sexual arousal, gratification, or abuse*. An accidental touch, or one without that purpose, doesn't meet the elements. So the book's claim holds: the accidental boundary crossing typically isn't a crime, while the deliberate threat of death can be.

- Verified: 2026-07-08
- Sources: [CALCRIM 960 — Simple Battery](https://www.justia.com/criminal/docs/calcrim/800/960/); [Gottesman Law — battery/sexual battery elements](https://www.anngottesmanlaw.com/criminal-defense/assault-battery/battery); general/specific intent doctrine (law-school casebook material)

## Q: Does the prosecutor really control charging everywhere in the US — is "pressing charges" fiction?

**A: Yes.** Prosecutorial discretion is settled US doctrine (Supreme Court, *Bordenkircher v. Hayes*, 1978: the decision whether to prosecute and what to charge "generally rests entirely in [the prosecutor's] discretion"). Victims don't file charges and can't drop them — a criminal case is the state versus the defendant; the victim is a witness. Victim cooperation heavily influences outcomes in practice (an unwilling key witness can gut a case), and many DV units run "no-drop" policies precisely because victims ask. The book's framing — you start a process, you don't steer it — matches the law.

## Q: Do mandatory-arrest rules exist, most commonly for domestic violence?

**A: Yes.** Roughly half of US states have some form of mandatory-arrest law for domestic violence: about 16 states plus DC require arrest whenever officers find probable cause of DV; another ~10 mandate it under specific conditions (visible injury, weapon, violation of a protective order). The rest use "preferred arrest" or officer discretion. Violating a protective order is often an automatic arrest trigger even in discretionary states. California is classified preferred/partially-mandatory. The book's phrasing ("in some places, certain situations — most commonly domestic violence — take the choice away from the officers") is accurate.

## Q: Does California require all parties' consent to record confidential conversations?

**A: Yes.** Penal Code 632 (Invasion of Privacy Act) makes it a crime to record a "confidential communication" without the consent of all parties — a communication where a party reasonably expects it won't be overheard or recorded (*Flanagan v. Flanagan*, Cal. Supreme Court 2002). PC 632.7 extends all-party consent to any cell/cordless call regardless of confidentiality. Violations carry criminal penalties and civil liability. Nuance a facilitator might care about: conversations in public gatherings, or where overhearing is reasonably expected, aren't "confidential" — so what counts in a workshop room depends on the circumstances.

- Verified: 2026-07-08
- Sources: [Cal. Penal Code § 632 analysis](https://rubinlawpc.com/criminal-laws/penal-codes/632-pc/); [Flanagan v.
