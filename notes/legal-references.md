## Q: Can anyone report a crime, or does the victim have to be the one who reports?

**A: Anyone can report a crime — victims, witnesses, or third parties with information.** No property rights, no victim status, no relationship to the event required. Whether police *investigate* and whether a prosecutor *charges* are separate downstream decisions (in a threats case the victim's account is often most of the evidence, so a victim who won't participate can end a case in practice — but the report itself is open to anyone). Some jurisdictions even formalize third-party reporting for sensitive crimes (e.g., Illinois 725 ILCS 203/22 for sexual assault; UK third-party reporting centres).

## Q: Can security physically remove someone, or can they only ask? (The bouncer question)

**A: Physical removal is legal, within limits — and the limits are general American law, not a special bouncer power.** The doctrine (common law, adopted broadly across states): the owner or lawful occupant of a property may use **reasonable, non-deadly force** to eject a trespasser, after the person has been asked to leave and refused within a reasonable time. Force may scale in proportion to the trespasser's resistance and the threat they pose, must stop when they're out (or comply), and may never be deadly just to remove someone — deadly force requires a separate self-defense justification (imminent death / great bodily injury). So the TV bouncer physically walking someone out is real law; the TV bouncer beating someone in the alley is battery.

**California version:** CALCRIM No. 3475 ("Right to Eject Trespasser From Real Property") — owner/lawful occupant may use reasonable force after a refused request to leave, if a reasonable person would see the trespasser as a threat to the property or occupants; force may increase in proportion to resistance.

**Industry practice** matches the legal shape: professional security standards are de-escalate, give clear notice and an exit path, keep any physical contact minimal (open-hand guidance, escort positioning), and **call police early when refusal continues** — because force past "reasonable" makes the guard the one committing a crime, and "reasonable" is decided later by a jury looking at the facts.

## Q: Do security guards have legal powers a regular person doesn't?

**A: No. A security guard is the property owner's agent, with the same legal powers as any other private person.** The uniform and badge add zero legal authority — California's own state training manual for guards (BSIS "Powers to Arrest") says exactly this: "A security employee has the same power to arrest as any other private person." What a guard exercises is the *owner's* rights (ask people their business, ask them to leave, use reasonable force on a refusing trespasser — see previous question) plus the citizen's-arrest power everyone has (in CA, Penal Code § 837: offense committed in your presence, or felony grounds). A citizen's arrest must be handed to police without delay, and impersonating a police officer is itself a crime. Practical upshot for an event: hiring security buys trained hands and a calmer ladder, not extra law — their ladder tops out at the same place yours does: the police.

## Q: Is California criminal threats (Penal Code 422) really chargeable as a felony?

**A: Yes — PC 422 is a "wobbler":** the prosecutor may charge it as a misdemeanor (up to 1 year county jail, $1,000 fine) or a felony (16 months / 2 / 3 years state prison, $10,000 fine), based on the circumstances and the person's history. A felony conviction is also a strike under California's Three Strikes law. The book's phrasing ("can be charged as a felony carrying prison time") is accurate — "can be," not "is."

## Q: Does criminal law really require intent — is an accidental boundary crossing typically not a crime?

**A: Yes, with the mechanism worth knowing.** Crimes require a guilty state of mind (*mens rea*). Battery requires a *willful* touching — an accidental, involuntary touch is not battery even if it causes harm (e.g., CALCRIM 960: "willfully... in a harmful or offensive manner"; California defense sources state directly: "If the contact was not willful, then it is not battery even if injury resulted"). Sexual battery (CA PC 243.4) goes further — it's a *specific intent* crime: the touching must be done *for the purpose of sexual arousal, gratification, or abuse*. An accidental touch, or one without that purpose, doesn't meet the elements. So the book's claim holds: the accidental boundary crossing typically isn't a crime, while the deliberate threat of death can be.

- Verified: 2026-07-08
- Sources: [CALCRIM 960 — Simple Battery](https://www.justia.com/criminal/docs/calcrim/800/960/); [Gottesman Law — battery/sexual battery elements](https://www.anngottesmanlaw.com/criminal-defense/assault-battery/battery); general/specific intent doctrine (law-school casebook material)

## Q: Does the prosecutor really control charging everywhere in the US — is "pressing charges" fiction?

**A: Yes.** Prosecutorial discretion is settled US doctrine (Supreme Court, *Bordenkircher v. Hayes*, 1978: the decision whether to prosecute and what to charge "generally rests entirely in [the prosecutor's] discretion"). Victims don't file charges and can't drop them — a criminal case is the state versus the defendant; the victim is a witness. Victim cooperation heavily influences outcomes in practice (an unwilling key witness can gut a case), and many DV units run "no-drop" policies precisely because victims ask. The book's framing — you start a process, you don't steer it — matches the law.

## Q: Is prosecutor-controls-charging true for small crimes too — or do victims of minor crimes have to pay/push to get anything done? (Author's hearsay check, 2026-07-08)

**A: Both halves are true, and they're about different things.** The *legal power* to charge belongs to the prosecutor at every level — misdemeanors included; Michigan's prosecutor association states it flatly: "ONLY the Prosecuting Attorney can issue or dismiss charges." No crime tier gives the victim that power. But the hearsay the author keeps hearing is real too — it's about *enforcement discretion*, not charging power: police have **no legal duty to investigate any given report** (no constitutional duty to protect specific individuals), and both police and prosecutors triage by severity. Documented example: Honolulu's prosecutor office closes some misdemeanor cases without review unless the complaining witness calls to follow up; an HPD captain on the record: "A lot of the responsibility falls on them, because without their assistance... our hands are going to be tied." So at the lower tiers, a report nobody pushes often ends at the report. Nobody legally *pays the police* to press charges (that would be bribery) — what people actually pay for when the system won't move is a private investigator or a civil lawsuit, which are different tracks entirely. Footnote: a handful of states (e.g., NC, PA, NJ, MA) let citizens file criminal complaints directly with a magistrate who can find probable cause — but the prosecutor still controls the prosecution after that.

## Q: Do mandatory-arrest rules exist, most commonly for domestic violence?

**A: Yes.** Roughly half of US states have some form of mandatory-arrest law for domestic violence: about 16 states plus DC require arrest whenever officers find probable cause of DV; another ~10 mandate it under specific conditions (visible injury, weapon, violation of a protective order). The rest use "preferred arrest" or officer discretion. Violating a protective order is often an automatic arrest trigger even in discretionary states. California is classified preferred/partially-mandatory. The book's phrasing ("in some places, certain situations — most commonly domestic violence — take the choice away from the officers") is accurate.

## Q: Does California require all parties' consent to record confidential conversations?

**A: Yes.** Penal Code 632 (Invasion of Privacy Act) makes it a crime to record a "confidential communication" without the consent of all parties — a communication where a party reasonably expects it won't be overheard or recorded (*Flanagan v. Flanagan*, Cal. Supreme Court 2002). PC 632.7 extends all-party consent to any cell/cordless call regardless of confidentiality. Violations carry criminal penalties and civil liability. Nuance a facilitator might care about: conversations in public gatherings, or where overhearing is reasonably expected, aren't "confidential" — so what counts in a workshop room depends on the circumstances.

## Q: If the threatened person won't participate but other people witnessed the threat, does the case die? Does it only take one person? (Author's question, 2026-07-22)

**A: No, it doesn't automatically die — and no, the threatened person's silence is not a veto.** Three separate findings. (1) **Anyone who heard it can testify.** A threats case can proceed on third-party witnesses, recordings, or texts; the threatened person's refusal weakens a case but doesn't end it as a matter of law. (2) **But one element lives inside the threatened person.** PC 422 requires that the threat actually placed them in *sustained fear* for their own or their family's safety — a subjective element. Courts hold this "may be proved with circumstantial evidence" and "inferred from the circumstances in which a crime is committed": what witnesses saw them do (fleeing, barricading, installing security, changing habits), plus their knowledge of the person's prior conduct. So it's provable without them, just harder. ("Sustained" is a low bar in practice — courts have found even very short durations sufficient where the person believed they were about to die.) (3) **Declining to participate isn't fully the threatened person's choice.** A prosecutor who needs the testimony can subpoena them — a court order, not a request; non-appearance can draw contempt or an arrest warrant. Prosecutors often *choose* not to compel a hostile or terrified witness, but the power exists. Net for the book: reporting is the threatened person's choice; what happens after is not, and one other witness is enough to start it.

## Q: Is it ever a crime *not* to report a crime you witnessed? (Author's question, 2026-07-22)

**A: Generally no — silence alone isn't a crime. But specific roles carry a legal duty that overrides personal choice.** Two separate bodies of law. (1) **No general duty to report.** The federal misprision-of-felony statute (18 U.S.C. § 4) is the closest thing, and courts require an *affirmative act of concealment* on top of knowledge — passive nondisclosure does not convict. There is no general legal duty to "snitch," and the Fifth Amendment overrides it where reporting would be self-incriminating. A handful of states have narrow duty-to-report statutes (typically for violent felonies witnessed in progress), but the default across the US is that not reporting is lawful. (2) **Mandated-reporter law is the real exception, and it's role-based, not event-based.** Every state designates professions that MUST report suspected abuse of protected groups. California runs two schemes: CANRA (Penal Code 11165.7, 40+ listed professions) for anyone under 18, and the Welfare & Institutions Code for elders (65+) and dependent adults (18–64 with physical/mental limitations). Listed categories commonly include licensed therapists and counselors, health practitioners, social workers, clergy, care custodians, and paid or unpaid caretakers. The trigger is *reasonable suspicion*, not certainty, and the duty runs regardless of whether the affected person wants a report made. **Book-relevant consequence:** a facilitator or staff member who holds one of those licenses may not get to set their own reporting threshold — and this is common in these spaces, where therapists and bodyworkers frequently staff events.

**AMENDED 2026-07-22 (author challenge — the first draft of this answer was too flat).** Two corrections. (a) **The duty is capacity-scoped, not 24/7.** PC 11166(a) triggers the obligation on knowledge gained "in his or her professional capacity or within the scope of his or her employment" — not on everything a licensee ever witnesses. Separately, the immunity provision (PC 11172(a)) extends protection to reports made on knowledge acquired *outside* professional capacity, and many institutions (e.g. UC system policy, Berkeley HR guidance) instruct their mandated reporters to report either way. So: institutional practice is often broader than the statutory duty. **The unresolved question for this book's readers is whether a licensed therapist staffing an event is acting "in professional capacity"** — genuinely ambiguous, and therefore a before-the-event conversation rather than a mid-crisis discovery. (b) **A few states impose universal reporting.** New Jersey and Wyoming require *all persons* to report suspected child abuse regardless of profession; Texas and Kentucky use similarly broad "any person" language. Roughly 48 states + DC and territories use the profession-designated model instead.

## Q: Does waiting to report kill the case? Is there a deadline, and do prosecutors drop things that have gone stale? (Author's question, 2026-07-22)

**A: There's a real deadline, and it's much longer than people fear — but the evidence decays long before the deadline does.** (1) **Statute of limitations.** California's general rule is 1 year for misdemeanors, 3 years for felonies. **Criminal threats (PC 422) carries 3 years regardless of which way it's charged** — the wobbler's misdemeanor version still gets the felony clock. **The mechanism (author challenge 2026-07-22, verified):** PC 805 states an offense "is deemed punishable by the maximum punishment prescribed by statute for the offense, regardless of the punishment actually sought or imposed," and PC 801 gives 3 years to anything punishable by state prison. So the clock keys to the *offense's* ceiling, not the *charging decision* — which is why a wobbler keeps the felony window either way. **Important limit on this: it does NOT generalize to all threatening conduct.** Behavior that could only ever be a straight misdemeanor (e.g. PC 415 disturbing the peace, PC 653m harassing/threatening communications) gets the ordinary 1-year misdemeanor window under PC 802. The 3-year figure belongs to the threat-to-kill-or-seriously-injure crime specifically. So "too much time has passed" is almost never literally true within the first couple of years. Tolling can extend it further (suspect leaves the state, conceals identity). (2) **But prosecutorial reality is the real constraint, and it's not a deadline — it's a slope.** Prosecutors may decline even with probable cause where there's "no reasonable likelihood of conviction," and the likelihood falls as witnesses disperse, memories blur, and contemporaneous corroboration (the 911 call, the same-night report, the people who were standing there) fails to exist. Combined with the triage finding above (minor cases closed without review absent a pushing complainant), a months-old report of a lower-tier threat is a weak case not because it expired but because there's less left to prove it with. **Net for the book:** the threatened person does not have to decide tonight — that pressure is false — but the account is worth more tonight than later, and the facilitator informing them should say both halves.

## Q: Can a facilitator hold the person who made threats on the property until police arrive? (Author's question, 2026-07-22)

**A: The power exists — and for this specific crime it is a trap.** California PC 837 lets a private person arrest for a public offense committed *in their presence*, or for a felony *not* in their presence. Two features make it dangerous here. (1) **For the not-in-presence route, the felony must actually have occurred** — a private person, unlike an officer, may not arrest on mere suspicion. And per the wobbler finding above, whether a criminal threat is a felony *at all* is the prosecutor's later charging decision. So the facilitator would be betting their own liberty on a determination nobody has made yet. (2) **Getting it wrong exposes the holder**, not the held: false imprisonment (PC 236), battery (PC 242), and in extreme cases kidnapping (PC 207); detaining longer than reasonably necessary is independently unlawful even where the arrest was justified. Practical layer on top of the legal one: holding converts a person who was leaving into a person who is fighting, inside a room full of people the facilitator is responsible for. **Net for the book: name it and steer away from it** — the container's authority is removal, not detention, and if the person is gone when officers arrive the report still proceeds on witnesses and documentation.
