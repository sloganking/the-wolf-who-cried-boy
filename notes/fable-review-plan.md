### A. Write "When It's Actually a Wolf" — new chapter (the clearest 10x move)

**The problem it solves:** The book teaches *verify, then respond proportionally* — but nearly every worked example in 162k words resolves as "it was a mistake." The scenario where verification confirms a genuinely selfish predator — and the community responds HIGH, hard, and *correctly* — is scattered across a few paragraphs but never gets a full chapter with the loving detail of Handling Threats of Violence. A hostile reader's strongest available critique is "this is a manual for making accusers doubt themselves." The critique is wrong (the under-response material, Repair Goes Both Ways, and fawner accountability prove the book's symmetry), but it is *available* — and one chapter closes it. It also completes the title's arc: the book thoroughly covers villagers who cry wolf at boys; it should show, once, in full, what to do when the wolf is real.

**Full brief written:** `notes/real-wolf-chapter-brief.md` — written by the reviewing session with the whole book in context; contains the fable-completion frame, section-by-section structure, verified cross-links, tone guidance, and open questions for the author. Start there.

**Approach (decided in conversation):**
- The chapter does NOT need deep selfish-predator psychology. The book already has the psychology: the blurry predator, "hungry and naive hunters," "selfishness is just unskilled need-seeking." The chapter's job is **protocol**: what verified malice looks like, how patterns across reports (which Appropriate Response already gestures at) turn isolated mistakes into confirmed predation, what a proportional HIGH-severity response looks like *executed without becoming a mob*, how a community removes someone cleanly, what to tell the group, what the paper trail looks like.
- Candidate example the author knows: a person whose actions were consistently selfish — possibly a fear response (manipulating when afraid). The chapter's stance can hold that ambiguity explicitly: you may never know whether it's fear or malice underneath, and the protocol is the same either way. You handle the *pattern*, not the diagnosis. That's actually on-message: the book keeps saying labels don't matter, behaviors and patterns do. Pattern only, never identifiable.
- Natural placement: "When Something Goes Wrong" section, likely after Before You Judge / Appropriate Response, or as the closing chapter of that section.

**⭐ THE MISSING METHOD (added 2026-07-25, author-originated; uncommitted proposal — he commits this repo himself).** The brief calls for *protocol*: what verified malice looks like, how patterns confirm predation, clean removal, the paper trail. It does not specify **how you verify without your own filter casting the deciding vote.** This is that, and it may be the chapter's spine.

**The starting-position rule:** *"If you see someone's actions and you start in certainty about what they are, you've already failed."* You cannot verify from inside a verdict. A first reading is one aperture, and it will feel complete — so the first move is not *look harder*, it's **start uncertain**, which is the only state in which looking harder gathers anything new.

**The shuffle:** throw the cards, let them land, look again — **three to five times**, each pass genuinely re-pointed. One shuffle beats zero (you actually tried to verify and concluded something, which is far better than an unchecked story), but one throw is still one aperture. **If all of them land on *this person is a selfish predator*, you have earned increased confidence.** The author's guard, which should survive into the prose verbatim in spirit: **confidence, deliberately not truth** — *"I'm not going to say truth, because some people don't know how to see and they're going to do it wrong."* **Reviewer's refinement to include:** a throw only counts *if the aperture actually moved.* Five looks from the same angle reaching the same conclusion is one throw reported as five, and it's more dangerous than never checking, because the story now carries a receipt. Test each pass: *what did I look at this time that I didn't last time?*

**Second reviewer's refinement — the shuffle has no stopping rule for innocence (added 2026-07-25, Opus 5 full-context read).** As specced above, the shuffle only says what to conclude when the passes *converge on guilt* ("if all of them land on selfish predator, you have earned increased confidence"). It says nothing about the two other outcomes, and both are more common: **convergence on innocence**, and **a split**. That asymmetry makes it a ratchet — and it will misfire in exactly the population this book worries about most, because a frightened person running five apertures will find the predatory reading on at least one, and fear weights the scariest aperture as the one that counted. Two rules close it: (1) **divergence is a result, not a failure** — mixed passes mean you do not have a verdict, and the correct output is continued protection plus continued uncertainty, not the scariest pass promoted to a conclusion; (2) **convergence on innocence obliges something too** — stand the protective posture down, and where that posture was visible to others, say so out loud, because an uncorrected protective stance silently becomes the accusation (the same mechanism as [the story that filled the silence](./before-you-facilitate.md#when-its-been-months), pointed at the verifier instead of the facilitator). Without both, the tool accumulates confidence in only one direction — the direction the rest of the book exists to slow down.

**The sequence — protect → shuffle → respond — and why it matters more than the shuffle itself.** The book's oldest unresolved tension is that *verify before you judge* sounds, live, like *stand there while someone gets hurt* — which is precisely why people skip it and swing. The author's resolution: **if there's enough uncertainty that there might be real danger, PROTECT, don't attack.** Get everyone away from the danger and into a safe position. **Then** shuffle. **Then** draw the sword if warranted, or walk with them as a friend. **Protection does not require a verdict — only the response does.** You can act instantly, decisively, at full strength on getting people safe while remaining completely uncommitted about who anyone is; separation, distance, ending a scene, walking someone out asserts nothing about their character, so it cannot be wrong the way a verdict can. And the mechanism underneath: **protection is what buys the time verification needs.** Three genuine re-looks can't happen in ten seconds; without protect-first, careful verification is a luxury nobody can afford mid-incident, so it gets abandoned on throw one.

**Prior art — what the book already has, stated accurately (author challenged an earlier, sloppier version of this note; corrected 2026-07-25 after a text check).** Two pieces exist and both are real:

1. **[Defense vs. Aggression](./before-you-facilitate.md#defense-vs-aggression)** is a **taxonomy** — a permission list of which actions are legitimate when something goes wrong. It explicitly includes *removing yourself from the situation* and *separating someone you care about from harm* as always-acceptable. Its rule: **defense protects, aggression punishes.** (An earlier version of this note under-described it as only "protecting is not punishing." It is more than that.)
2. **This walkthrough's own Order of Operations** — remove the endangered person *first*, before adjudicating anything, because **safety comes before principle** — is a genuine **sequence**, and it is this rule in action.

**What is genuinely missing, and it is not the pieces — it is the join.** A taxonomy tells you which moves are *legal*. A sequence tells you which order to play them in **and why the order works**. The book never states the connective principle: **protection does not require a verdict — only the response does.** Therefore you may act immediately and at full strength on safety while remaining entirely uncommitted about who anyone is; and **protection is what buys the time verification needs**, because three genuine re-looks cannot happen in ten seconds.

**Three verified findings from a text check (2026-07-25), which make this a placement problem as much as a content one:**

- **`appropriate-response.md` states the sequence as two steps with nothing in between:** *"The responsible person verifies first, then responds."* Verify → respond. There is no protection step, and nothing anywhere in that chapter about what to do with live danger during the verification window.
- **`before-you-judge.md` never addresses it at all.** Every occurrence of "protect" in that chapter is about *other people wrongly believing they are protecting* (Inquisitors, rescuers, the accuser who protects nobody because they never verified). A frightened reader is told to slow down and is never told what to do about the danger while they do.
- **Defense vs. Aggression is linked from exactly two places — the conflict-specialist paragraph and the facilitator checklist — both facilitator-facing.** It is never linked from either chapter where a scared *participant* is being asked to hold off on judging. **The answer exists in the book and is sitting in a room that reader may never enter.**

**So the recommendation is two-part and both halves are cheap:** (1) state the connective principle once, plainly, where the sequence lives; (2) surface it in `before-you-judge.md` and `appropriate-response.md` — even a single sentence plus a cross-link would close it, e.g. *"If there might be real danger while you're still figuring out what happened: protect first. Getting people to safety asserts nothing about who anyone is, which is exactly why you can do it before you know."* This is the gap that makes "verify first" read as unusable to a frightened person, and it is why they skip it and swing.

### SS. The police chapter is a different epistemic genre — maintain it like a spec (added 2026-07-25, Opus 5 full-context read)

**The finding.** `where-the-police-fit.md` is the strongest new material in the facilitator half, and it is also **the first chapter in the book that can be factually wrong.** Every other chapter makes unfalsifiable claims of a specific useful kind: *here is a mechanism, check it against your own experience.* A reader can disagree; they cannot fact-check it. This chapter cites statute sections (Penal Code 422, 243.4), counts jurisdictions ("about sixteen states and DC" for mandatory arrest, "roughly ten more" conditionally), asserts limitations periods and how the clock is set, characterizes citizen's-arrest exposure, and states recording-consent splits. Those are a different species of sentence, and they carry two costs nothing else in the book carries:

- **Correction risk.** One stale number is the only thing in 181,000 words a hostile reader can *prove* wrong — and proving one factual claim wrong is worth more to them than disagreeing with fifty mechanisms. ### G. Two-edition strategy (solves length without lobotomizing)

Do NOT prune the web hyperbook — its redundancy is what makes each page standalone, which is correct for its form. Core ideas (fear creates what it fears; responses have severity; fawning mechanics; show-don't-tell) are each explained fresh in four-plus chapters; that serves web readers and taxes linear ones. The move: keep the maximal hyperbook as-is, and create a **condensed print edition (90–110k words)** via a dedicated pass that treats the linear reader as the client. This also solves the "13.5-hour ask" for every future reader, not just facilitators.

**Trim criterion for that pass (added 2026-07-01):** use the purpose razor from Considerations — *does this passage change what the reader sees or does around the moment something goes wrong?* — as the cut/keep test, alongside the standing pruning rules (net value, not conciseness; redundancy is a lesser sin than lost value; keep consolidation lists).

**Second trim rule — the redundancy comes in two kinds and only one is cuttable (added 2026-07-25, Opus 5 full-context read).** Read linearly, the repetition isn't evenly distributed, and the distinction matters more than the percentage estimate:

- **Forward-referencing scaffolding — cut this.** A concept gets re-taught inline because the chapter is about to use it: fear-creates-what-it-fears, show-don't-tell, the fawning mechanics, response-has-severity. In a hyperbook that's what keeps each page standalone; in print it's a cross-link. This is where the volume is.
- **Positional rotation — do NOT cut this.** The *same* concept re-derived from a different position: fawning from the fawner's side, then the partner's side, then the facilitator's side, then the room's side. Top and bottom vulnerability seen from the top, then the bottom, then the facilitator who was never taught to look. This looks identical to redundancy from outside and it is the book's actual method — the rotation is what produces the "oh, that's me" moment, because most readers only recognize themselves in one of the positions.

**Why this needs to be in the brief:** an editor — or a model — running a dedup pass without this rule will cut the second kind, because at paragraph level it's indistinguishable from the first. The test is one question: *is this the same explanation, or the same mechanism seen from a different chair?* Same explanation → cut. Different chair → keep, even in print.

### X. Law-enforcement section for `handling-threats-of-violence.md` + jurisdiction checklist (added 2026-07-04, author-initiated; 7x+)

**Source:** a second full-book read (all ~168k words in one context window, Fable 5, 2026-07-04) plus a conversation with the author. **The gap:** Handling Threats of Violence (rated 9.5, "liftable whole") walks through a death-threat crisis without ever mentioning the option of calling the police — including for the unanswered failure mode: *what does Step 3 look like if the violent person refuses to leave?* A facilitator has no enforcement mechanism beyond social authority; the walkthrough currently ends where that authority ends. A community that never involves law enforcement *no matter what* is running "we handle things internally" — the same institutional pattern that shielded abuse in churches and organizations. The book is uniquely positioned to thread it: **internal handling for mistakes, external backstop for crimes** — proportionality applied to institutions.

**Why nobody calls (candidate teaching, from the author's own realization):** it's usually not a decision — it's an unavailable option. Three mechanisms: (1) *we use the tools we're aware of* — people who have never used the police for anything don't have them filed as a tool, so the option never surfaces under pressure; (2) the room's inverted severity map disables the tool — nobody calls the police on the person the room believes is the hero; (3) the target's own filter generalizes — someone with a misunderstood-and-attacked filter (the book's Person B / Target pattern) who has just been publicly attacked doesn't experience the legal system as a remedy but as *more people who will misunderstand and attack me*, and the room's social proof (an audience seeming to agree, nobody protesting) confirms it. The same filter that magnetizes the attack files the remedy under "threat" (the existing "terrified of a legal system that was pointed at the person threatening you the whole time" passage — cross-link, don't duplicate). An ill-informed room can't use a tool correctly even when everyone privately disapproves of what's happening. De-named observation available: people outside a container's story, hearing these events secondhand, reliably ask "why didn't anyone call the police?" — the distance between that question and the room's actual behavior measures how deep the under-response norm runs. (Personal-material gate: any first-person framing must pass the Incident Disclosure Canon sign-off in the private context; related private material exists — see private context.)

**Verified facts to carry (verified 2026-07-04; keep the "this isn't legal advice" register of the existing law section):**
- **"Pressing charges" is a myth everywhere in the US.** Victims don't file or drop charges; prosecutors do. Calling the police starts an investigation the caller doesn't control: officers assess probable cause → removal, arrest, or report on file → prosecutor decides. Calling ≠ jail (arrest ≠ charges ≠ conviction; weak threat cases are routinely declined, and a victim's non-cooperation often ends them — but can't guarantee it). This dissolves both fears at once: "if I call, they're definitely ruined" and "I can fully control it later." Neither is true.
- **There's a middle option:** "someone is refusing to leave my property" is a trespass/removal call, distinct from "I want to report criminal threats." What you lead with shapes but doesn't determine the outcome — officers who find probable cause for felony threats can act regardless.
- **The shape is invariant; the weights vary.** Every US state criminalizes this (criminal threats / terroristic threats / menacing / intimidation); classification (felony vs misdemeanor), arrest likelihood, mandatory-arrest provisions (mostly domestic-violence contexts), and protective-order procedures differ by state — and everything differs internationally.
- **The honest trade-off, named not hand-waved:** police arriving at a sex-positive event is itself a container event — privacy and outing risk, attendees with rational reasons to fear police contact, venue relationships, and in some jurisdictions questions about the event's own legal standing. That's *why* these communities under-call. It changes the design (decide thresholds before the crisis), not the conclusion.

**Structure (follow the item-D tiering):**
- **Principle:** law-enforcement thresholds are the *fire exits* of a container — decided before the event, hopefully never used; not knowing where they are isn't purity, it's negligence. The call is itself a response with a severity: for imminent danger it's defense; after the fact, it's a choice that belongs to the threatened person, informed and unpressured.
- **Full protocol:** pre-decide in The Promise (e.g., "imminent physical danger → we call 911, no debate; threats without imminence → documented immediately, the threatened person decides about reporting, we support either choice"). Facilitator duties: document (the book already teaches this — note that a room full of witnesses is what makes a threats case provable), inform the threatened person of their real options including the press-charges myth, and pressure in neither direction (pressuring to report = Rescuer; pressuring not to report to protect the org = "When Protection Goes to the Wrong Person").
- **Minimum version:** one sentence in the opening agreements ("threats of violence end your place here, and may also be a police matter we will not shield you from") + the facilitator privately knowing the trespass-vs-report distinction and the local emergency/non-emergency numbers.
- **The jurisdiction checklist (the author's addition):** a scannable pre-event list — figure these out for the state/country where you're hosting, the way you'd locate the fire exits: (1) what the threat crime is called there and whether it's felony or misdemeanor; (2) emergency + non-emergency numbers for the venue's location, and realistic response time (rural retreat ≠ city); (3) trespass law — what it takes to have someone removed from the property, and *who* has standing to ask (venue owner vs event host: know your venue agreement); (4) any mandatory-arrest provisions that remove discretion once police arrive; (5) emergency protective/restraining order options and how fast they move; (6) who controls charging (everywhere in the US: the prosecutor — know the myth); (7) recording-consent law for documentation (one-party vs two-party states — California is two-party); (8) for international retreats: the local law on threats, the event's own legal standing in that country, and how local police relate to events like yours. ### Z. Cross-community pattern reporting — "whisper network" ethics (added 2026-07-06; two-model convergence)

**Source:** raised independently by a Sonnet 5 model doing a fresh full-book read (2026-07-06) with no prior exposure to this document — it found the gap by reading the book itself, then cross-checked against this file and confirmed the gap wasn't already covered. Independent convergence with this document's own findings (which never named this specific gap) is itself worth noting — it wasn't one session's blind spot.

**The gap:** every facilitator-facing chapter (`before-you-facilitate.md`, `handling-threats-of-violence.md`, `walking-your-talk.md`) assumes a single coherent container with one identifiable authority. Real containers don't stay contained — someone removed from one organizer's retreat can show up at a different organizer's event next month — and the book has no protocol for whether or how that information should travel between facilitators. This is the book's own central tension (`before-you-judge.md`: verify with primary sources, don't act on secondhand accounts) recurring at the inter-organizational level, where the standard tool for resolving it — go talk to the people involved — usually isn't available. The book has already built the single-container version of this move (`appropriate-response.md#report-to-facilitators`, `punishment-culture.md#protected-reporting` — "someone who makes mistakes repeatedly... changes the assessment," reports as information not verdicts) but never extends it past one facilitator's boundary.

**Why it's harder than the existing items:** the book's machinery for avoiding witch hunts (verify with primary sources) was built for a facilitator with direct access to the people involved. A facilitator hearing about someone from a *different* community is usually several hops removed — exactly the "human telephone" problem `before-you-judge.md#primary-sources-matter` warns against. So the tool meant to prevent a witch hunt is least available exactly where the risk of one is highest. Any answer has to hold both directions of the book's asymmetry at once: under-warning lets a genuine repeat pattern move unchecked between communities (the mechanism `punishment-culture.md#who-stays-and-who-leaves` already shows can hollow out a single scene, now operating between scenes); over-warning imports mob dynamics into a channel with *less* due process than one container has, and reputational harm that crosses communities has no single room to walk back into and demonstrate change in front of — the trust-rebuilding logic in `i-made-a-mistake.md#what-actually-rebuilds-trust` requires an audience that can see you over time, and a whisper network has no audience, only recipients.

**Candidate shape (unresearched — needs its own thinking pass before drafting, not a first-principles page written from a to-do bullet):** likely the item-D/item-X tiering — principle (a name that's crossed the "documented pattern, not a single incident" threshold in one container is information other facilitators are entitled to have, on the same logic as protected reporting inside one); full protocol (facilitator-to-facilitator only, never routed through participants; a stated verification bar — something like "documented pattern with repair declined or attempted and failed," not "someone told me"; the person discussed gets a chance to respond before it spreads further, preserving something like due process outside a formal container); minimum version (a private, facilitator-only channel with an explicit bar, rather than either silence or open community gossip).

**Placement:** likely a new subsection in `before-you-facilitate.md`, cross-linked from `punishment-culture.md#protected-reporting` and `before-you-judge.md#primary-sources-matter`. Natural pairing with item X as "external backstops the internal protocol can't provide" — X is the legal backstop, this is the inter-community one.

**Status: OPEN.** Not scoped, not drafted. More structurally novel than most items on this list — deserves a dedicated thinking session before it becomes a chapter, not a quick answer bolted onto an existing page.

### UU. Punishment culture at dyad scale — the person closest to an over-responder can't bring them their own harm (added 2026-07-27, author-originated; FF's sibling)

### HH. The Repair Wall — a show-don't-tell artifact for the anti-punishment culture (added 2026-07-22, author-originated; assess ~7x)

**The idea (author's, 2026-07-22):** a physical/visible artifact in a container — imagine a large chart or wall — that displays every **rupture→repair pair** the container has produced: two clasped-hands symbols plus the two participants' first names (or initials/a symbol, for the private), one entry per pair who had a rupture *and* both later felt good enough about the repair that they **chose** to mark it there, for others to see. "The last 233 people repaired." Opt-in, and framed as paying it forward — *if you rupture and repair, your name can be here too, showing the next person it's possible.* The author's hunch: it would re-orient a whole community around mistakes and concretely build the OPPOSITE of a [punishment culture](../src/concepts/punishment-culture.md).

**Why it likely clears the bar (mechanism — this is `punishment-culture.md` + The Promise made physical):**
- **It's the book's own show-don't-tell method turned into an object.** The chapter argues a learning community forms when people *witness* mistakes handled well ("that becomes the model for what happens when you mess up here"). The wall is that witnessing, made standing and countable instead of dependent on being in the room the day it happened. Telling people "mistakes are normal" is a prescriptive norm and barely moves behavior; showing 233 completed repairs is a *descriptive* norm, the far stronger lever.
- **It makes the invisible denominator visible.** Shame survives because people only ever hear the catastrophic cases; a wall of hundreds of quiet repairs makes "rupture is normal, repair is the usual outcome" a fact you can count, not a reassurance you have to believe.
- **It pre-loads the repair schema.** Before a participant's own rupture ever happens, they've already seen hundreds of arcs end well — so when theirs hits, the nervous system reaches for a template with a known good ending instead of a cliff. This is the fear-creates-what-it-fears loop run in reverse: expecting repair makes people more willing to attempt it.
- **It's [The Promise](../src/concepts/before-you-facilitate.md#the-promise) proven, not just stated.** Instead of the facilitator *saying* "we handle things proportionally, we won't destroy you over an honest mistake," the wall is standing evidence it has happened N times. And adding your name flips the valence of having ruptured — from a thing to hide into a contributor's/elder's badge (pay-it-forward), the exact inversion of the punishment culture's "get caught = annihilated."

**Design cautions the passage must carry (dilution/harm guards — mostly from the book's own material):**
- **Consent is the whole game.** Opt-in only, both parties, per pair; offer initials or a neutral symbol for anyone who wants the record without the exposure. Some ruptures involve real harm you would NOT memorialize as two cute clasped hands — the wall is for mutually-completed repairs the participants *chose* to publish, never a default.
- **Guard against survivorship bias becoming a new obligation.** A wall of only successes must not imply repair is guaranteed or pressure fawn-repair / premature reconciliation. The book already separates repair from coerced reconciliation ([`repair.md#ask-for-what-you-actually-want`](../src/concepts/repair.md)), and "sometimes the clean, honest answer is a no or an exit" has to stay explicitly honored, so *not* being on the wall never reads as failure. (Cross-link the `before-you-facilitate.md` survivorship-bias material — the same reasoning that says "staff can play, with systems" says "show repairs, with the not-everything-repairs truth kept visible.")
- **It's primarily a facilitation ritual; the book's job is to describe the artifact as a technique.** Likely home: a short subsection/sidebar in **Punishment Culture** (right where Protected Reporting + "what a learning community looks like" already live) or **Before You Facilitate**, describing the device + the consent design + the cautions, as one more concrete tool a facilitator can build. Also a signature move for the coaching/facilitation practice (logged author-side in `../RPM/capture.md` #32).

### II. Post-incident support practitioners — aftercare as an industry standard (added 2026-07-24, author-originated from the phone desk; assess 7x+, example-first)

**The idea (author's, 2026-07-24):** containers and retreats in this field should have dedicated **post-incident emotional-support practitioners** — aftercare/crisis specialists available when the work harms someone emotionally, trained for the injuries *native to this field*: going along with something while fawning, not saying no in the moment, feeling violated afterward, being in the room for a public blow-up. Present it example-first: the author's own incident is the motivating case — a container full of shaken participants after public screaming and an inverted accusation, and no support structure of any kind; everyone, most of all the most-harmed person, left to metabolize alone.

**Design elements (author's, same session):**
1. **Specialized, not generic** — practitioners for this class of injury, not general talk therapy.
2. **Third-party by design** — hired from outside the organization, so the support is never the org protecting itself (the failure mode to design against: the in-house counselor whose job quietly becomes convincing the hurt person they weren't harmed).
3. **The economics are trivial** — raise the ticket $100–200/person and it's funded; the "how could we afford that" objection dies on arithmetic.
4. **Tiered response** — group processing for the whole container after a public event; individual support for the directly harmed. Open design question: does the person who *committed* the harm receive support too, including when the acts were criminal-level?
5. **Lived-experience requirement** — the counselors must have attended such containers themselves, ideally know boundary-crossing from the inside. You can't counsel an experience you've never had.
6. **Industry side effect** — creates well-paid roles for skilled, underpaid practitioners; the author's larger observation: the industry is full of unsolved problems, and problems are opportunity.

**Why it likely clears the bar:** this is the systems-change family the book already runs — Protected Reporting, the designated incident specialist (item Z), the Repair Wall (HH) — and aftercare is the missing member: Z covers *competence during* the incident, HH covers the *culture after repair*, this covers the *harm in between*. "I could see this becoming the industry standard" is the claim the passage would make, and the book's authority to make it is that it documents exactly what the absence cost. Candidate home: `before-you-facilitate.md` systems material, adjacent to item Z.

**The affordability objection, answered by shown arithmetic (author, 2026-07-24, second installment):** the passage should pre-empt the overwhelmed reader ("crisis experts, training, staffing... how am I going to afford all this complexity?") by SHOWING the math, not asserting affordability. One worked example, kept generic: a seven-day transformational retreat at ~$2,400/ticket raises the price $200 — roughly 7–8% — and a 30-person container just funded ~$6,000 per event: enough to train crisis specialists, have one present, and pay for months of post-retreat counseling for a participant whose experience went sideways. (Tension to manage: the book stays general, but one concrete worked example is the show-don't-tell; keep it as an illustration, not a prescription.)

**The ROI case — show the value, not just the cost (author, 2026-07-24; flagged by him as prediction, "my story, a little theoretical" — write it as reasoned prediction, never as fact):** organizations in this field often carry damaged reputations precisely because of how they handle incidents, and their leaders' biggest fear is public attack on the org. Aftercare inverts that: crises handled well + hurt participants actually supported → the author's prediction: an order-of-magnitude fewer detractors, because most detractors are made in the unsupported aftermath. Participants who feel safe say "I had a hard moment and they caught me," return, and bring friends — so the ~7% price rise plausibly INCREASES attendance and income rather than costing it. The deeper line available to the passage: the thing organizations fear most (reputation attack) is largely a product of the aftercare they declined to fund.

**Role separation (author, 2026-07-24, third installment):** the facilitator and the aftercare-holder are different jobs, and the passage should say so. Facilitators are skilled at holding space and leading the container; they are not necessarily the best at holding someone's distressed, traumatic emotions after something goes wrong — and the person whose job that becomes doesn't always want it forever. The facilitator still shows up (as the face who held the space, that's responsible presence), but a specialist carries the emotional aftermath. This also answers the "we already handle it" objection: they're under-resourced for it, and handling-it-well is its own expertise.

**Sequencing for the passage (author):** prevention first — the book is already full of preventative systems, and that's step one; the aftercare layer is explicitly step two, for the incidents prevention doesn't catch. Frame it that way so aftercare never reads as a substitute for building the container right.

**The near-verbatim candidate line + the blind-spot question (author: "I fucking love this line"):** *"the thing organizations fear most is largely a product of the aftercare they declined to fund"* — with his correction folded in: it isn't always *funding* declined; sometimes the idea is simply absent. Open analysis thread for the draft: WHY don't orgs do this already? Candidates: the mental-accounting flinch below (paying for aftercare feels like losing money to a bad event), fear-creates-fear (planning for harm feels like admitting harm happens), or plain blindness (nobody wrote the standard down before). Worth a paragraph, not a chapter.

**The safety-fund psychology (author, third installment — a real design problem for the passage):** not every container needs its aftercare budget spent, so the ~$6K/event either accumulates as a standing incident fund (it can even compound — protecting victims AND the org's legal/reputational flank), or gets conditionally taken as profit in good years. His key design requirement either way: **the money must be pre-categorized as "the safety money"** — because if an incident's costs come out of what the org thinks of as profit, the psychology becomes "something bad happened and now I'm losing money, maybe I'll just not do the aftercare," which is the exact failure that creates detractors. Earmarking beats good intentions; the mechanism question (never-pocket fund vs. conditional release) stays open.

**Fourth installment (author, 2026-07-24, late):**
- **The "wellness bonus" reframe (the WoW rested-XP move)** — a candidate answer to the safety-fund mental-accounting problem above: World of Warcraft rebranded the same mechanic from a *deficit on normal days* to a *boost you earn* — identical math, opposite psychology. Applied here: in good years the unspent safety money is announced as the **wellness bonus** — "everything went well, so we earned it" — instead of incident years reading as "I'm losing money." Same dollars, but the org now WANTS the container to go well to earn the bonus, and aftercare spending never feels like a fine. Placement question flagged by the author: main book vs. a facilitator-facing companion vs. author-side only — assess with the item.
- **Announce aftercare at the container's OPENING (author's design rule, adopted for his own containers):** aftercare is stated up front, near The Promise — "if something happens and you feel upset, we have counselors; you can ask" — not discovered by the hurt person after the fact. Unannounced aftercare fails half its job: the safety is created by the existence AND the announcement, and the hurt person must already know they're allowed to ask. This composes directly with The Promise material in `before-you-facilitate.md`.
- **The term is "aftercare" (author's endorsement).** Native vocabulary — the community already uses "aftercare" for post-scene care, so the book extends a word readers own rather than coining one.

**Fifth installment (author, during the law-enforcement review pass — dated after the fourth above):**
- **The bonus is an incentive loop, not just a reframe.** The fourth installment solved the *backward-looking* problem (aftercare spending shouldn't feel like a fine). The author's addition: if the unspent safety money is released as a bonus only when containers go well, the org acquires a direct financial stake in incidents not happening — which turns safety procedure from a cost center into a revenue lever. His framing: an org that notices *"I earned half the bonus this year because half my containers blew up"* now has a reason to revisit its procedures, invest in prevention, or hire someone who builds the system. That's the forward-looking half the passage was missing: the money doesn't just stop punishing good behavior, it starts paying for getting better. Rough scale he's working with: ~7% added cost per container (~$6K on his working figure) covering specialist training and aftercare providers.
- **The failure mode to design against (flagged in-session, must ship with the idea):** a bonus paid for "no incidents" incentivizes *suppressing incident reports*, not preventing incidents — the well-documented underreporting dynamic in industrial safety-bonus programs. Whatever metric releases the money has to reward **handling quality** (was it caught, named, handled by protocol, documented, aftercare offered) rather than **silence**. An org that quietly buries an incident to earn its bonus is the exact detractor-manufacturing machine this item exists to prevent. This caveat is not optional garnish — a reader who adopts the mechanism without it builds the opposite of what's intended.

- **Hold the payout — "nothing happened" isn't known at container close (author, same pass).** A bonus released when the container ends is paid on incomplete information. Someone can surface months later: *"a boundary got crossed, I didn't speak up at the time, and I need support now."* That isn't an edge case — it's the pattern this book documents everywhere else (freeze and fawn mean the people most harmed are frequently the least able to say so in the room, and a container that ends quiet may be quiet for exactly that reason). The author's instinct: defer the release 3, 6, or 12 months, so the money is still sitting there when a late disclosure arrives. Two design bonuses fall out: it removes any incentive to hustle a container to "clean" status, and it composes with the underreporting caveat above — a lagged fund can't be earned by burying what hasn't surfaced yet. **Open: which lag.** Long enough to catch late disclosures, short enough that the incentive still feels real to the org — and possibly tiered (partial release early, remainder at the far marker).

- **Matched-spend variant, considered and set aside (author floated it, analysis in-session).** The idea: the org receives bonus money only equal to what it spent helping people — spend $600 on someone's therapy, unlock $600. The author's own worry (it incentivizes creating harm then paying for it) is the *weaker* objection: at 1:1 the org nets **zero** and still absorbs the incident's operational and reputational cost, so there's no profit in manufacturing harm. The real flaw is directional — **zero incidents pays zero bonus**, so the best possible year is the least rewarded one, which reinstalls the exact "bad things cost me money" frame the WoW reframe was built to remove. Matched spend does capture one genuine benefit: it makes aftercare *free* rather than merely pre-budgeted.
- **The general tension to design against (name it in the passage):** money that reaches the org's pocket motivates behavior and can be gamed; money that never reaches the pocket can't be gamed and doesn't motivate. The lag above is a partial answer. Candidate synthesis — a **tiered waterfall**: (1) aftercare costs reimbursed first, so spending is never experienced as a loss (matched spend's benefit, without its inversion); (2) surplus rolls into prevention next — specialist training, hiring, protocol work; (3) only what survives both becomes profit, released on the lag. Keeps the "we earned it because it went well" psychology while making the good year genuinely the profitable one.

**Sixth installment (author, 2026-07-26) — two additions from the MM.2 design session:**

- **Funded choice beats org-selected third party.** Design element 2 above ("third-party by design — hired from outside the organization") does not fully close the capture problem it names: a practitioner the org selects and pays is still selected and paid by the org. The author's variant: **the harmed person picks their own practitioner and the org funds it to a stated cap.** Removes the org from the loop entirely while still paying, and it is the strongest available answer to the in-house-counselor failure mode. Keep the org-provided option too — some people want the easy default — but the funded-choice path is what makes the offer trustworthy.
- **Counselors trained in advocacy — and the separation that makes it safe.** The author's proposal: practitioners trained not only to support but to help the person *notice when they aren't advocating for themselves*. His own answer to the obvious objection (isn't that against the org?) is the right one: an incomplete matter becomes a public grievance later, so completion is the org's interest too. The addition that makes it work structurally: **eliciting the demand and granting it are separate jobs.** The counselor's job is helping someone find out what they actually want; the org's job is answering — and "no, but here's what we can do" is a real answer the book already has machinery for (the gift of no). This is also the sharpest argument for funded choice: a practitioner the org neither selects nor pays can advocate without a conflict of interest.
- **Lessons-page candidate, sequenced.** The author asked whether *"the thing organizations fear most is largely a product of the aftercare they declined to fund"* belongs on `lessons.md`. Per item LL, that page is claims the book **earns**, in teaching order, each linked to the chapter that earns it — so the line has nothing to link to until this item ships. Good candidate once drafted (rare in being aimed at operators rather than readers); not before.

**Status: OPEN — not yet drafted; gate on the 7x+ assessment with the incident example leading.** Author-side ledger: the same idea is recorded for his own future containers in `../RPM/school-vision.md` §6 and pointed from `../RPM/capture.md` #32.

### KK. The Magnifying Glass — the METHOD for assembling sight (added 2026-07-25, author-originated; likely absorbs item Y; assess 7x+)

**Status: uncommitted proposal.** Drafted into this file at the author's direction during the Opus 5 full-context review night; the author commits book-repo changes himself.

**The insight (author, 2026-07-25, recorded before the language faded):** intelligent beings are each a **magnifying glass**. A few bits of input balloon into thousands of neurons firing, so you look at a few shades of red and *the whole brain lights up red* — and that is what you think the thing **is**. Look at a different part of the same object: "oh my God, it's all blue." Same object, different aperture, total conviction both times. The new claim, and it goes past *seeing is a process*: you are **not able** to see all of someone or something at once. Not "you fail to" — the aperture is structurally too small. Whole-picture perception is not on the menu.

**So the method — the only way to see someone somewhat accurately:** (1) spend time across multiple *separate* occasions; (2) deliberately move where attention lands each time; (3) **memorize** each aspect; (4) assemble the memories into a map you can zoom out on. Accurate sight is never perceived. It is **constructed**.

**The kicker line:** *"Whether you love them or hate them at first glance, you have no idea whether you love or hate the full picture."*

**Why it likely earns its place — it fills a hole the book currently leaves open.** The book teaches the *diagnosis* in at least three places and never gives the reader a *procedure*: "Everything Has Everything In It" (`trauma-and-filters.md`), **factually selective**, and the blind men and the elephant. All three end at *your view is partial*. None tells a reader what to do about it alone, over time. More pointedly: **The Mirror closes the entire book with "the only way to decrease blindness is to always assume you're blind to something and look for it"** — an instruction with no method attached. This is the method. That makes the strongest candidate placement the Mirror itself, where it would upgrade the book's final answer from an exhortation into something a reader can execute Monday morning.

**⭐ REVISED SAME NIGHT — the vehicle should be the elephant, not the magnifying glass.** The author found a stronger carrier a few hours later, and it does not add a metaphor to the book, it **completes one the book already tells.** This supersedes the magnifying glass as the book-facing version (the glass stays in his private notes as the underlying mechanism).

`trauma-and-filters.md` currently ends the blind-men parable where every telling ends: *they're all correct about what they observed, they're all wrong about the whole picture.* A diagnosis with no exit. The extension, near-verbatim from the author:

> **You'd have to touch the whole damn elephant in order to know what the elephant is.** And then — assuming the elephant doesn't move — even though you're a conscious being who can only touch, and therefore perceive, one part at a time, **you can know what the whole elephant is and where you are relative to it.** *Oh, it feels like a snake, but this is the trunk, so the full elephant must be right there.*
>
> **That's what seeing clearly actually is.** Not that you've transcended the human condition. Not that your attention is wider than anyone else's. **You built a map, and now you know what's there.**
>
> And then: *I'm at the left foot, so the right foot must be about three feet over.* You reach. You touch it. And someone says **"how did you know that? That was so insightful."**
>
> **Because I've been here before. My aperture isn't actually wider than yours. Where you only have sight, I have insight — because I've used my sight to look at everything possible to look at here, before.**

**Why this version and not the glass:**

1. **It subsumes instead of adding.** The pileup guard below was the main risk; extending a parable the book already tells eliminates it entirely. No new furniture.
2. **It gives the parable an ending.** The blind men have been a diagnosis for two thousand years. *Touch the whole thing* is an exit.
3. **It's bodily rather than optical** — hands, not lenses — which is the book's native register.
4. **⭐ It is the only safe form of the accumulation argument.** A separate open question (author's private notes, `coaching-core.md`) concluded that "why my sight is better than yours" is door material, never book material, because the book earns authority by *conceding* status and a superiority claim would be its most off-key page. **This solves it.** *"My aperture isn't actually wider than yours"* is an explicit denial of superior perception; the advantage is located entirely in **reps on the same terrain**, which is verifiable, humble, and implies the reader gets there too. It explains why the author sees fast without ever claiming a faculty — and that makes it publishable where the other framing wasn't.

**Placement, revised:** (1) **directly after the blind-men parable in `trauma-and-filters.md`**, extending it in place — strongest, because the setup is already written and the reader is already holding the image; (2) the conclusion's Mirror section can then *reference* it as the method behind "always assume you're blind to something and look for it," rather than carrying the whole idea; (3) `what-clear-eyes-are-for.md` is a natural echo — that chapter is about what sight is *for*, this is what sight *is*.

**Keep the human-condition framing with it, and keep it non-pejorative:** we perceive a small amount at once and blow it up into a massive brain, so the fragment fills the whole brain and therefore *looks like* the whole thing. Not a flaw, not stupidity — simply what being a conscious being is like. That framing is load-bearing for the political application below, which fails instantly if it reads as calling anyone dumb.

**Original placement analysis (kept for the record, now superseded):** (1) the conclusion's Mirror section; (2) `trauma-and-filters.md` adjacent to "Everything Has Everything In It"; (3) a short standalone in *Seeing Clearly*; (4) as the frame for item Y rather than a separate home.

**It likely absorbs item Y.** Y has been open since 2026-07-04 as "find a home for *the truth is so often the opposite of what we first expect*" — and it has been hard to place precisely because a line alone arrives as a thesis announced in advance. It is not a slogan looking for a home. It is the **punchline of this mechanism**, and the author's own favorite working question is its operational form: **"How is the truth actually the opposite?"** — the question that finds the mountain of gold behind you when you were only seeing the pile of gold in front of you. Recommend evaluating Y and KK together, with Y's line as KK's closing beat.

**Guards, and they matter:**

- **Do not let the book become epistemology.** This must arrive as a short, concrete, procedural passage attached to material that already exists — not a new philosophical chapter. The book's power is that it stays close to rooms and bodies.
- **Metaphor pileup risk.** The book already runs the RAS/brown-red exercise, the blind men and the elephant, and the horror-movie effect on this exact terrain. "Magnifying glass" is currently unused anywhere in `src/` (checked), so the term is free — but a fourth perception metaphor needs to *replace* load-bearing work, not sit beside it. Consider whether the magnifying glass should become the umbrella the elephant hangs under, rather than an addition.
- **State the neuroscience as metaphor, not mechanism.** "Five bits balloon into thousands of neurons firing" is a loose claim. Either source it or phrase it as image. The book already carries soft-source exposure elsewhere; this is a cheap place not to add more.
- **The political application probably stays out.** The author's framing scales the idea to factions — *"somebody looks at one part of the tree and sees red, somebody another part and sees blue... you're all right, you're just all blind"* — and it is genuinely strong, non-partisan, and explains conviction without calling anyone evil or stupid. But the book deliberately chose 500-year-old examples **so the reader's filters don't fire while they learn the mechanism** (stated outright in The Mirror). A live political example would trigger exactly the filters the design avoids. If used at all, keep it fully abstract ("factions," never named ones) and place it *after* the mechanism has landed, not as the illustration that teaches it. Author's call.

**Provenance:** author-originated during the 2026-07-25 review night; full entry and lineage in the private repo (`core-psychology.md` → The Magnifying Glass; `distinctions.md` → Belief & perception). Parent law: *seeing is a process, not a state* (May 2026). The author's note on its weight: **it is his working answer to "how do I gain sight?" — the first procedural one he's had**, which matters because sight is the book's central promise.

### MM. Where the bar sits for "get the threatened person out first" (added during the law-enforcement review pass, author-raised)

**The question (author):** the Order of Operations tells the facilitator to remove the threatened person before anyone else. Does that hold for every threat of violence, or only for death threats? His framing: *"Threatening to beat them up would just be like, have them leave, right? #### MM.2 — Reopened 2026-07-26 (author). The departure/decision split. NOT DRAFTED; design settled, two questions open.

**Why this reopened — what the shipped protocol doesn't handle.** The author's counter-case: *"if you touch her, I'll beat you up."* Fully physical, so the shipped rule sends the attacked person home — and he doesn't think it should. Chasing that produced a better diagnosis than a lower bar: **Step 1 fuses two decisions with completely different time horizons, and forces both at the speed of the faster one.**

> "I need you to leave the event right now, for your safety... **Go home.**"

- **Getting out of range** — the next five minutes, while the violent person is still in the room. Cheap, reversible, and the geometry argument holds regardless of severity.
- **Going home** — the next five days. Expensive, hard to reverse, and it has nothing to do with the next five minutes.

Because the sentence welds them, the facilitator cannot move someone out of danger without also ending their week — and every attempt to find a clean severity threshold fails, because no seconds-scale rule can answer a days-scale question. That is the whole reason the bar felt arbitrary. It also explains a defect the author independently flagged from the receiving side: the removal reads as expulsion because, as written, it *is* one.

**The plan as it stands.**

1. **Separation is unchanged and unconditional.** Physical threat → the attacked person leaves the site immediately, with an escort, before anyone addresses the violent person. The author's original ordering rationale stands untouched: telling a threatener to leave while the target is present risks the lash-out the sequence exists to prevent. An earlier version of this proposal weakened this to "out of the room" — **rejected**, author-caught: at Step 3 the target would be forty feet down a hall instead of traveling, which is exactly the moment maximum distance is worth most.
2. **Leaving is not expulsion.** The only change to Step 1: the script stops pre-committing a decision nobody can make yet. Working draft, author-endorsed ("it would feel good to receive this"): *"I need you to get away from here right now, for your safety. You're not in trouble. Someone's going with you. Once this is handled I'll find you and we'll figure out the rest together."* The vagueness of "once this is handled" is deliberate and author-ratified — the chapter already promises facilitators may act before explaining, and a script that pre-writes a sequence breaks the moment reality diverges.
3. **Whether they return is decided later, and the decision is split by who can actually know.** The **facilitator owns a physical-safety veto** — can access be controlled for the rest of the event (perimeter, entrances, staffing, response time, cell reception)? Only they know the venue. The **participant owns everything else** — a narrative-locked crowd, the shame, whether any of it is worth it. Only they know what they can carry. Not arbitrary: each side decides the thing the other cannot see.
4. **The veto is not paternalism** (author's own argument): a returning threatener endangers the whole container, so the risk isn't confined to the person choosing. A participant may consent to risk to themselves; they cannot consent for thirty other people.
5. **Severity re-enters as a dial, not a line.** Higher severity raises what the perimeter must defend against. Most containers cannot screen for weapons, so most venues honestly fail at death-threat level — which is where the author's instinct already sat, but now with a reason instead of a rule, and it correctly handles both edges (a death threat at a guarded venue; a promised beating on open public land).
6. **No return logistics get built.** Preserving the option costs nothing: nobody waits nearby (that parks the threatened person in the same area as the threatener, possibly while he's driving around), nobody shuttles. They go where *they* choose with the escort. The entire difference between "go home, you're done" and "get somewhere safe, we'll talk" is one sentence not being said.
7. **Containment gets defined** (this is item QQ arriving at the same place): "contain" currently tells a facilitator nothing. It means **move the violent person to a separate room with someone on them** — a far smaller ask than "get off the property," so much less likely to trigger the lash-out Step 2 exists to avoid, and it buys separation anyway. Name QQ's unstated second purpose here too: every minute of containment is distance.
8. **Ask what they need.** The strongest argument is mechanical, not moral, and should be the one the book gives: *the people most likely to be harmed in these spaces are the least likely to state a demand*, and the book has a chapter on why. A protocol that responds only to stated demands systematically under-serves exactly the population it was built for. Closes `advocacy-gap.md` item 8, extended backward from the follow-up to the incident.

**Escort selection (author-raised, 2026-07-26 — must ship with the design).** The escort cannot be narrative-locked against the person they're escorting, and cannot be someone that person finds threatening. An escort who believes they're guarding a predator delivers the container's verdict in private, at the person's worst hour, with no witnesses — and a physically intimidating escort re-runs the threat the escort exists to end. This makes item NN (accompaniment) load-bearing rather than optional, and connects it to the existing filter-diversity and staff-composition material: *who is available to do this* is a staffing decision made before the container, not a grab in the moment.

**Containment staffing — who watches the violent person (author-raised, 2026-07-26; the mirror of escort selection).** The author's worry: the watcher should be friendly enough to keep the person calm, but not so friendly they collude — narrative-locked together, and the person gets let loose. He flagged he didn't know where the line sits, and floated "neutral." **Recommendation: don't calibrate friendliness at all — it has no findable setting. Three binary tests instead:**

1. **Can they say no to this person?** The disqualifier isn't warmth, it's inability to refuse — the existing [friction check](../wiki/friction-check.md). A warm person who can hold a line is the *best* watcher, because rapport is what keeps containment from escalating into the violence Step 2 exists to avoid. A warm person who can't refuse them is the worst. The failure mode is fawning to someone frightening, not friendship.
2. **Do they share the story?** Exact mirror of the escort constraint: the escort fails when narrative-locked *against* the person they protect; the watcher fails when narrative-locked *with* the person they contain. One defect, two directions — worth stating as a pair, since it teaches the general principle (a staff member running the room's story cannot hold either role).
3. **Do they know the departure plan?** They shouldn't. Item QQ already establishes destination/route/timing as the withholdable facts. So the design doesn't have to assess anyone's loyalty — **compartmentalize instead: the watcher is not given the plan.** Collusion cannot leak what it never had.

With those three in place, friendliness stops being a risk and becomes an asset. All three are pre-container staffing decisions, alongside the conflict specialist and the escort — which is the same convergence item Z already names: a container's safety is whether the right knowledge is in the right hands before the moment arrives.

**Why NN is now a component, not a companion.** The escort is simultaneously the communication channel at venues without cell reception ("I'll call you later" fails silently), the mechanism by which the return conversation happens at all, and the answer to someone sitting alone ten minutes away.

**Open questions — author's call, both flagged by him:**
1. **How long the escort stays, and what it costs a small staff team** to lose a person for an evening. NN assumes accompaniment; it never prices it.
2. **Whether containment always means a separate room**, or whether venues without one need a weaker definition.

**Also raised, tracked elsewhere:** the facilitator-side ROI of eliciting demands early — the unspoken grievance compounds, and the fawner who says nothing leaves with a story that surfaces later as a public accusation or a filing. Author: *"helping them advocate for themselves is protecting you as well."* Home identified: `walking-your-talk.md` → "It's All Risky," which already dismantles *"caving is safer"* with an accounting argument rather than a moral one; this is the same argument extended past the incident. See item II for the aftercare/counselor half.

### OO. Can an organization build a friendly relationship with local police *before* it needs one? (author-raised as a side thought, law-enforcement pass; researched in-session)

**The author's question:** can an org make itself a known, non-suspicious entity ahead of time so a call gets a better response — *"Hey, I'm a known entity, I run a transformational sexual retreat, people are known to get their emotions high"* — or does that backfire into scrutiny? He flagged not knowing, and that it likely varies by place.

**Researched answer (2026-07-22), three findings:**
1. **The channel already exists and is bureaucratic, not social: the special-event permit.** Many jurisdictions require one, and departments run dedicated special-events units (Las Vegas Metro's is a lieutenant, two sergeants, ten officers, three coordinators; Houston, Rockford, NYC, DC equivalent). Permit conditions commonly require a **named on-site liaison with authority to stop the event** — which maps directly onto the book's conflict specialist. Going through it makes you a known cooperative organizer as a *permit applicant*, not as someone volunteering information about sexual content.
2. **Community/LGBTQ+ liaison officers are a real second channel — but be precise about why they exist.** They were not created for anything resembling sex-positive events. They exist because police were historically the *source* of harm to LGBTQ people: bar raids and entrapment. SFPD appointed the first US liaison to the "homophile community" in 1962, and that officer's work included ending entrapment of gay men in public restrooms; Chicago, Dallas, DC and many others run programs now, with the stated purpose of helping people from historically-targeted communities feel able to report. **What transfers to this book is the structure, not the history:** a population with rational reasons to avoid police contact, and a department-side channel built to bridge exactly that — which is the situation the chapter already names ("attendees with rational reasons to fear police contact"). If this ships, do not imply the programs were built for events like the reader's; that overstatement was made in-session and corrected. Williams Institute research notes effectiveness varies sharply with departmental support — so it's a real door, not a guaranteed one.
3. **The cheapest relationship is one the reader may already have: the venue's.** A retreat center operating for years is already known to the local sheriff with a track record the organizer didn't have to build. Inheriting it is lower-risk than constructing one, and it's a natural addition to the venue conversation checklist item 3 already sends them to.

**The framing rule that makes this safe to publish:** build the relationship through the mundane logistics every event has — capacity, parking, medical plan, on-site contact, quiet hours — never through disclosing erotic content. That's a complete and honest picture of a private adults-only retreat, and it doesn't hand anyone a reason for vice interest or create a record framing the event around sex. **Do not write this as a blanket recommendation:** in a jurisdiction with active vice enforcement or hostile zoning the calculus inverts, and the book has no way to know the reader's locale — which is precisely the variable checklist items 1 and 9 already teach them to go find.

**Where it would go:** `where-the-police-fit.md` — either a new Local-Law Checklist item ("who to talk to before you host, and what to route through them") or a short subsection after the checklist. Sources for the file if it ships: LVMPD/Houston/Rockford special-events units, [Williams Institute — LGBTQ liaison officers](https://williamsinstitute.law.ucla.edu/publications/lgbtq-liaison-officers/), [LGBTQ liaison officer history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBTQ_liaison_officer). **Not yet entered in `legal-references.md`** — it's practice rather than law, but any claim about what departments do should be logged there before shipping.
