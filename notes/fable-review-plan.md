## Overall Assessment

- **Body of work: 9/10.** The conceptual architecture is a 10 — the book manufactures missing language (righteous predator, top/bottom vulnerability, narrative lock, sinsickness, blurry predator, power debt, belief-blindness, two victims problem) and explicitly understands why that matters: the linguistic gap creates the perceptual gap. The book also *practices its own teaching* at scale — inarguable language, show-don't-tell, the Kinsey passage catching the reader's verdict in both directions, the conclusion's Mirror turning the book on the reader. The medium is genuinely the message, sustained across 160k words.
- **Web edition ship-readiness: 8.5/10 — it is legitimately shipped.** The cross-linking makes the length a feature; nobody has to read it linearly.
- **Trade paperback/ebook readiness: 6.5–7/10.** Needs a structural edit for linear reading (deduplication), quote-permissions check, and one legal/sensitivity pass before print.
- **No fatal problems.** Two structural issues (the missing "real wolf" chapter; length/repetition for linear formats) and one hot spot (the apology dissection in Repair). All solvable. Details in the to-do list.

### Industry impact prediction

On content: this is the most complete treatment of mistake-handling, false accusation, and conflict in sex-positive spaces that exists. There is no competing text. Betty Martin gave the industry the Wheel of Consent; the ISTA lineage gave it RBDSMT; *The Ethical Slut* gave non-monogamy its vocabulary. Nobody has given the industry a framework for what happens when something goes wrong — the single area where these communities need the most help. Concepts like top vulnerability, The Promise, protected reporting, and the threats-of-violence walkthrough are the kind that migrate into facilitator trainings and become infrastructure. "Bottom vulnerability has a safeword. Top vulnerability doesn't" is the sentence facilitators will repeat to each other. Ten years out, "top vulnerability" being a term facilitators use without knowing where it came from is a realistic outcome — that's what industry-defining looks like.

On trajectory: content doesn't determine adoption — champions do. The books that defined this industry got there through workshop pipelines and teacher endorsements, not shelf presence. Realistic best path: a handful of respected facilitators adopt two or three named tools, teach them, credit the book, and the vocabulary spreads sideways. As-is: cult-classic potential within the niche. With the polish moves below plus facilitator endorsements plus a condensed field edition: a genuine shot at becoming the standard reference for incident handling in these spaces — with later crossover into workplace/community conflict, because the frameworks scale down.

### A. Write "When It's Actually a Wolf" — new chapter (the clearest 10x move)

**The problem it solves:** The book teaches *verify, then respond proportionally* — but nearly every worked example in 162k words resolves as "it was a mistake." The scenario where verification confirms a genuinely selfish predator — and the community responds HIGH, hard, and *correctly* — is scattered across a few paragraphs but never gets a full chapter with the loving detail of Handling Threats of Violence. A hostile reader's strongest available critique is "this is a manual for making accusers doubt themselves." The critique is wrong (the under-response material, Repair Goes Both Ways, and fawner accountability prove the book's symmetry), but it is *available* — and one chapter closes it. It also completes the title's arc: the book thoroughly covers villagers who cry wolf at boys; it should show, once, in full, what to do when the wolf is real.

**Full brief written:** `notes/real-wolf-chapter-brief.md` — written by the reviewing session with the whole book in context; contains the fable-completion frame, section-by-section structure, verified cross-links, tone guidance, and open questions for the author. Start there.

**Approach (decided in conversation):**
- The chapter does NOT need deep selfish-predator psychology. The book already has the psychology: the blurry predator, "hungry and naive hunters," "selfishness is just unskilled need-seeking." The chapter's job is **protocol**: what verified malice looks like, how patterns across reports (which Appropriate Response already gestures at) turn isolated mistakes into confirmed predation, what a proportional HIGH-severity response looks like *executed without becoming a mob*, how a community removes someone cleanly, what to tell the group, what the paper trail looks like.
- Candidate example the author knows: a person whose actions were consistently selfish — possibly a fear response (manipulating when afraid). The chapter's stance can hold that ambiguity explicitly: you may never know whether it's fear or malice underneath, and the protocol is the same either way. You handle the *pattern*, not the diagnosis. That's actually on-message: the book keeps saying labels don't matter, behaviors and patterns do. Pattern only, never identifiable.
- Natural placement: "When Something Goes Wrong" section, likely after Before You Judge / Appropriate Response, or as the closing chapter of that section.

**Resource note (2026-07-06):** a consultant/reader with directly relevant lived and professional experience (real-predator survivorship, crisis work, long underground-community background) is available to the author for this chapter — who it is and the consent rules for the ask live in the gitignored `wiki/.private-context.md`, and the full five-advantage plan (headline: a survivor-side inarguability audit — the one test the book cannot run on itself — plus the brief's open question 2, section-2 pattern verification, a crisis-work pressure-test of the conflict-specialist material, and the acknowledgment question) plus the hire-as-paid-consultant recommendation live in the author's private RPM notes. ### D. Retier the hard facilitator prescriptions: principle / full protocol / minimum viable version

The horror-before-temple rule and feed-yourself-first are currently stated as blanket mandates ("you don't get to work my staff"). The author has since built better nuance elsewhere — the survivorship-bias section shows that "staff can play, with systems" beats a blanket ban, and the same logic applies here: "you watched horror so you can't staff" is the blanket-ban shape, and it needs updating to match. The state check (formerly "Gun Check") already models the right pattern ("some containers may prohibit play after failing; others may allow it consciously").

**The fix for each hard prescription:** present the **principle** (a primed RAS / a hungry animal body is a liability), the **full protocol** (the rule as written), and the **minimum viable version** (e.g., an arrival check-in: "have you consumed anything recently that contained violence, or that upset you?" — then assess, with a buffer staff member available to take over). The check-in version catches the *mechanism* rather than one input — it catches the news-doomscroll case the horror rule misses — and treats staff as adults.

**Voice constraint (decided):** do NOT soften these into pure open questions. The industry isn't doing any of this; the book's value is that someone finally wrote down a complete design. "Here is a full system — calibrate it to your container" is a stronger gift than "here are some things to consider." Keep the authority; add the gradations. Show options; don't dilute into question marks.

**Also tier the refund section** (`handling-threats-of-violence.md#the-refund-show-dont-tell`). It's one of the book's strongest moves — show-don't-tell applied to money — but it's written in pure command voice ("Refund the mistake-maker." / "Do not refund the violent person."), while the adjacent "Tell the Crowd" subsection already models the right register ("this is a judgment call, but I lean toward transparency"). Tier it: **principle** — trust is rebuilt by costly action; "removal was protection, not consequence" needs material proof. **Full protocol** — as written. **Minimum version** (currently missing): the text skips a real constraint — small facilitators run thin margins and a full retreat refund can genuinely hurt. Credit toward a future event, a partial refund, or at minimum a public explicit statement that the removed person's standing is unchanged (the zero-dollar version) all honor the principle. Note for context: the *prospective* policy (refund issued during crisis handling) and *retroactive* repair (reopening an old incident with a refund attached) are very different asks — the book only makes the first; don't judge the prescription's adoptability by how facilitators respond to the second. **Prime Field Review Packet question:** would you refund? What would stop you? What's the version you'd actually run?

### E. Make the staff "hungry ghost" vibe check procedural

Currently "you can feel it" — squishy in a book that is otherwise objective and solid. It can be made procedural with the book's own Notice tool: describe what a camera would record. The tell isn't a feeling — it's an **attention-allocation pattern**: whose needs is this staff member's attention serving? Observable markers: casting a wide net across many participants and lingering longest where personal interest lies; conversations that track the staffer's desire rather than participants' needs; attention that follows attraction around the room while on duty. Plus a self-check question set for staff, and a check-in framing for leads.

**Candidate example (author's own, shared in conversation — approved perception, his call on final wording):** the author was an *assistant* at a retreat — paying to attend, with a discount in exchange for assisting — where assistants were permitted to play with participants if the facilitators approved. Important nuance for writing it: this is NOT a rule-breaking story. Assistants there wear two hats — on-duty (helping, cleaning, prepping the space, being the person participants come to) and personal free time. The issue was attention allocation: during the container, his attention was often more on finding a sexual connection — working through conversations with each woman until she tired of talking, then the next, seeing who was interested, and orbiting those the most — than on service. He worked hard AND the hunger cast a wide, visible net. The lead facilitator named it as "hungry ghost energy"; he couldn't see it at the time. That's the whole teaching: hungry ghost energy is invisible from inside, visible from outside — which is exactly why the check must be external and behavioral, not self-report. It also shows the check isn't about policing whether staff seek connection (the container allowed it) — it's about whether hunger is driving attention *while on duty*. Anonymized/first-person; don't name the school or the facilitator in connection to this story.

### X. Law-enforcement section for `handling-threats-of-violence.md` + jurisdiction checklist (added 2026-07-04, author-initiated; 7x+)

**Source:** a second full-book read (all ~168k words in one context window, Fable 5, 2026-07-04) plus a conversation with the author. Author rates the jurisdiction checklist "7x plus — at least in an addendum, if not in the main chapters, where it would probably be more likely to be seen." Recommendation: main chapter, with the checklist as a boxed/scannable list inside it.

**The gap:** Handling Threats of Violence (rated 9.5, "liftable whole") walks through a death-threat crisis without ever mentioning the option of calling the police — including for the unanswered failure mode: *what does Step 3 look like if the violent person refuses to leave?* A facilitator has no enforcement mechanism beyond social authority; the walkthrough currently ends where that authority ends. The book already establishes (`when-youve-been-wronged.md#know-where-the-law-actually-stands`) that in these scenarios the threats are typically the only crime in the room — and then the facilitator playbook handles a felony entirely internally. A community that never involves law enforcement *no matter what* is running "we handle things internally" — the same institutional pattern that shielded abuse in churches and organizations. The book is uniquely positioned to thread it: **internal handling for mistakes, external backstop for crimes** — proportionality applied to institutions.

**Why nobody calls (candidate teaching, from the author's own realization):** it's usually not a decision — it's an unavailable option. Three mechanisms: (1) *we use the tools we're aware of* — people who have never used the police for anything don't have them filed as a tool, so the option never surfaces under pressure; (2) the room's inverted severity map disables the tool — nobody calls the police on the person the room believes is the hero; (3) the target's own filter generalizes — someone with a misunderstood-and-attacked filter (the book's Person B / Target pattern) who has just been publicly attacked doesn't experience the legal system as a remedy but as *more people who will misunderstand and attack me*, and the room's social proof (an audience seeming to agree, nobody protesting) confirms it. The same filter that magnetizes the attack files the remedy under "threat" (the existing "terrified of a legal system that was pointed at the person threatening you the whole time" passage — cross-link, don't duplicate). An ill-informed room can't use a tool correctly even when everyone privately disapproves of what's happening. De-named observation available: people outside a container's story, hearing these events secondhand, reliably ask "why didn't anyone call the police?" — the distance between that question and the room's actual behavior measures how deep the under-response norm runs. (Personal-material gate: any first-person framing must pass the Incident Disclosure Canon sign-off in the private context; related private material exists — see private context.)

**Verified facts to carry (verified 2026-07-04; keep the "this isn't legal advice" register of the existing law section):**
- **"Pressing charges" is a myth everywhere in the US.** Victims don't file or drop charges; prosecutors do. Calling the police starts an investigation the caller doesn't control: officers assess probable cause → removal, arrest, or report on file → prosecutor decides. Calling ≠ jail (arrest ≠ charges ≠ conviction; weak threat cases are routinely declined, and a victim's non-cooperation often ends them — but can't guarantee it). This dissolves both fears at once: "if I call, they're definitely ruined" and "I can fully control it later." Neither is true.
- **There's a middle option:** "someone is refusing to leave my property" is a trespass/removal call, distinct from "I want to report criminal threats." What you lead with shapes but doesn't determine the outcome — officers who find probable cause for felony threats can act regardless.
- **The shape is invariant; the weights vary.** Every US state criminalizes this (criminal threats / terroristic threats / menacing / intimidation); classification (felony vs misdemeanor), arrest likelihood, mandatory-arrest provisions (mostly domestic-violence contexts), and protective-order procedures differ by state — and everything differs internationally.
- **The honest trade-off, named not hand-waved:** police arriving at a sex-positive event is itself a container event — privacy and outing risk, attendees with rational reasons to fear police contact, venue relationships, and in some jurisdictions questions about the event's own legal standing. That's *why* these communities under-call. It changes the design (decide thresholds before the crisis), not the conclusion.

**Structure (follow the item-D tiering):**
- **Principle:** law-enforcement thresholds are the *fire exits* of a container — decided before the event, hopefully never used; not knowing where they are isn't purity, it's negligence. The call is itself a response with a severity: for imminent danger it's defense; after the fact, it's a choice that belongs to the threatened person, informed and unpressured.
- **Full protocol:** pre-decide in The Promise (e.g., "imminent physical danger → we call 911, no debate; threats without imminence → documented immediately, the threatened person decides about reporting, we support either choice"). Facilitator duties: document (the book already teaches this — note that a room full of witnesses is what makes a threats case provable), inform the threatened person of their real options including the press-charges myth, and pressure in neither direction (pressuring to report = Rescuer; pressuring not to report to protect the org = "When Protection Goes to the Wrong Person").
- **Minimum version:** one sentence in the opening agreements ("threats of violence end your place here, and may also be a police matter we will not shield you from") + the facilitator privately knowing the trespass-vs-report distinction and the local emergency/non-emergency numbers.
- **The jurisdiction checklist (the author's addition):** a scannable pre-event list — figure these out for the state/country where you're hosting, the way you'd locate the fire exits: (1) what the threat crime is called there and whether it's felony or misdemeanor; (2) emergency + non-emergency numbers for the venue's location, and realistic response time (rural retreat ≠ city); (3) trespass law — what it takes to have someone removed from the property, and *who* has standing to ask (venue owner vs event host: know your venue agreement); (4) any mandatory-arrest provisions that remove discretion once police arrive; (5) emergency protective/restraining order options and how fast they move; (6) who controls charging (everywhere in the US: the prosecutor — know the myth); (7) recording-consent law for documentation (one-party vs two-party states — California is two-party); (8) for international retreats: the local law on threats, the event's own legal standing in that country, and how local police relate to events like yours. Cross-links: The Promise, the emergency exception, `#document-what-actually-happened`, `#know-where-the-law-actually-stands`.

Also mandatory field-guide material (item G's sibling products).

**Guardrails:** no new incident facts beyond the canon without author sign-off. Keep the existing chapter's stance intact — this section adds the backstop, it must not turn the walkthrough into "call the cops first" (the internal protocol remains the primary path for everything below imminent danger).

### AA. Does the book communicate the falsely accused person's REAL victimhood? (added 2026-07-06, author-raised; for the pre-production full-context review + field packet + survivor reader)

**The trigger:** in-session, an AI summary phrased the book's hostile reading as "written by an accused man; it minimizes real victims." The author caught two things. (1) The phrase itself repeats fiction — the accurate description is: the author was attacked by a righteous predator (public predator-labeling, spread stories, death threats) and is a *real victim in the top-vulnerability direction*. "I was called a predator" makes listeners wonder; "someone attacked my reputation and called me names" gets empathy — the author has observed this difference directly in his own anonymous disclosures. (2) The deeper question: the book says "mistake maker" constantly — does its language under-communicate that the person on the receiving end of a false accusation is a first-class victim of a first-class harm, not merely a mistake-maker who got over-responded to? Everyone's bias runs against seeing that victimhood — which is part of why the book exists.

**Author's candidate coinage:** "top victim / bottom victim," parallel to top-and-bottom vulnerability. **Session recommendation: don't coin it — but audit the emphasis.** Reasons against the coinage: (a) The Way Out ("Your Language Tells You Where You Are") deliberately moves the wronged reader *out* of victim-identity vocabulary as its healing mechanism — installing "top victim" as an identity term the reader adopts cuts against the book's own machinery; (b) the writing guide prohibits person-labels in favor of described mechanisms; (c) the structural claim already exists in the book's concepts: the Two Victims Problem, the severity model (reputation attack = HIGH/permanent), top-and-bottom vulnerability. If there's a failure, it's emphasis and directness, not missing machinery. The distinction that reconciles the tension: **being a victim of a real harm (a fact the community must name and act on) vs. living in victim identity (a state the harmed person graduates out of).** The book asks the *community* to fully recognize the first while coaching the *harmed person* out of the second — it should do the first as loudly as it does the second.

**Status (2026-07-06): a git branch exists attempting the "mistake-maker" fix, needs Fable 5 review.** Goal for this item: rename "the mistake-maker" → "the attacked person" throughout the book (primarily `handling-threats-of-violence.md`, plus cross-references in `walking-your-talk.md` and both glossaries).
