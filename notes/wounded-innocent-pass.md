# Wounded-Innocent Language Pass

> **Status:** FINDINGS, not edits. Nothing under `src/` was changed. Every entry is a proposal for the author's read.

**The brief (the author's words, 2026-09-23):** *"I was playing wounded innocent baby boy in the language, find all those."* The targets are passages where the prose pleads for the author, or for the accused, to be seen as innocent, good or okay, when it could stand on ground the book actually holds: proportion, verification, observable events and mechanism.

**The worked example (already fixed in `the-rescue-that-made-me-see.md`):**

- **Before:** "Every person is a human being — not a monster, not a label. And when people can see that ... they're free."
- **After:** "Some people do monstrous things. Even then, what they're owed is what anyone is owed: someone who checks what actually happened, and a response the size of what they actually did."
- **Why:** the old line argued a claim anyone could dispute (nobody is a monster), and it read as a plea to be seen as innocent. The new line stands on proportion and due process. Those apply to everyone, including the person who attacked him.

**Markers searched for:** innocence verdicts in the author's or the accused's favour ("falsely," "wrongly," "innocent," "weren't true," "didn't deserve"); pain inflated to win sympathy ("destroyed"); sweeping absolutions ("good people," "not a monster"); passages that ask the reader to side with the accused instead of showing the mechanism; and self-descriptions whose job is to look pure.

**Deliberately not flagged:** owned mistakes and self-implicating stories (the one second, the hickey, "I used her to abuse myself"); plainly stated feelings (anger, grief, "it hurt me deeply," the panic attacks); and the book's real teaching that most harm comes from fear rather than malice.

**Method:** fourteen fresh readers, each an Opus model with one chapter or a small group of chapters, read every line of `src/` against this brief. Findings are compiled below in `SUMMARY.md` order. Each quoted passage was checked verbatim against its source file, and rewrites were checked to make sure they add no incident facts beyond what `src/` already says.

### Top 10 by impact

Impact here means how visible the passage is, how directly it pleads, and whether the reader will say it out loud (scripts rank highest).

1. **When You've Been Wronged, Show Your Humanity script:** *"I believe all people should be treated as human beings, not monsters."* This is the worked example's twin, and it's worse, because it's a script the reader says aloud to a hostile room and the section calls it "inarguable." → *"I believe anyone accused of something deserves someone checking what actually happened, and a response the size of what they actually did."*
2. **Conclusion:** *"Treating every person you interact with as a human being instead of a monster."* The same line survives in the book's closing argument. → *"Checking what actually happened before you decide what someone is. Answering with a response the size of what they did, even when your fear wants it bigger."*
3. **When You've Been Wronged, When Someone Comes to You Afterward:** *"I'm innocent."* This is a scripted innocence claim in the chapter that tells a reader with a MEDIUM mistake to own it. → Tell them what you did, what you did to repair it, and what the other person said afterward.
4. **Handling Threats of Violence, What to Say to the Crowd:** *"it was good-faith, and they did nothing that warrants removal."* A facilitator certifies innocence in public, which breaks the page's own rule two paragraphs later ("say what you have, not what you've concluded").
5. **Types of Mistakes, A Personal Example:** *"I only want to create good things... I'm completely disinterested in crossing boundaries."* This is the purity self-description almost word for word, sitting inside an otherwise well-owned mistake.
6. **All Power Is Mutual, Selfless Defense:** *"The largest reason I did it was for them — and for everyone who would come after them."* It outranks the honest self-interest in the line just before it with the noblest possible motive, which no reader can check.
7. **Healing Fawning, Your Touch Is a Privilege:** *"My reputation can be destroyed based on her lie about her own consent."* One sentence carries an innocence verdict, inflated pain and an underdog plea.
8. **Before You Facilitate, Where I Stand:** *"including being falsely accused."* The book's model paragraph for declared ground carries a verdict in the author's favour. "Including being the one accused" holds the same ground.
9. **About the Author:** *"I've been accused of things I didn't do."* A plain innocence verdict on first contact, which is exactly what Don't Lead With the Verdict warns about.
10. **Repair, When Apologies Don't Land:** *"They spread stories about me that weren't true."* This one is filed under the book's own label "That's the Notice." A camera can't record "weren't true."

**Also high, just outside the ten:** Why Rescuers Are Dangerous teaching *"a righteous predator falsely accused and attacked me"* as the reader's coping sentence; Trauma & Filters giving a real ex-partner a motive (*"She was training me... by punishing me"*); Before You Judge's *"a mistake by a good person"* as "the reality," in the chapter that argues against deciding before verifying; I Made a Mistake's *"It doesn't see your goodness."*

### Patterns across the book

These recur, so a single decision can fix many lines at once.

- **The "not a monster / human being" absolution** shows up at least eight times beyond the fixed line: Wronged script, Conclusion, the Rescue (father), All Power Is Mutual (principle), Walking Your Talk, I Made a Mistake ("saw a monster where there was a human being"), From Threat to Ally ("good people"), and Before You Judge (Inquisitors). The worked example's move, proportion and due process, fits almost every one.
- **The one-word verdict:** "falsely," "wrongly," "innocent," "weren't true," "didn't deserve," "clearly didn't do." Most of these fixes are just deleting the word. The sentence already describes the mismatch in size and doesn't need the ruling.
- **"Destroy/destroyed" as the cost of being accused:** Healing Fawning, Before You Judge, Appropriate Response, All Power Is Mutual, Before You Facilitate, Walking Your Talk. The observable cost (pushed out of a community, carried for months, a response sized for something else) is plainer and lands harder.
- **"A good person"** added to the book's true fear-not-malice teaching. "It's not malice. It's a mistake" holds. "A mistake made by a good person" is a character reference.
- **The reader silently switched to "wrongly accused innocent."** This happens in chapters addressed to someone who did make a mistake (I Made a Mistake, When You've Been Wronged, Own Your Part). The reassurance is written for the innocent and read by the person who erred.
- **Downstream copies:** The Lessons quotes chapters word for word. Fix the source chapter and the Lessons line follows.

### How the opposite is true (read before accepting findings wholesale)

This was one aperture: a hunt for a single pattern. A hunt finds what it hunts for, so the list over-reads. Three counterweights:

1. **The book's thesis is real ground.** Mistakes do get over-punished, and the book shows this mechanism for 160,000 words. The writing guide's own boundary says that deep in a chapter, after the ground is built, naming a false accusation as false is *earned*. Many low-confidence findings sit exactly there. Where a sentence is inside a stipulated example ("Person B did nothing"), the verdict belongs to the example, not to the author.
2. **"Cry victim" is defended terminology** (writing guide, Defended Terminology). These findings don't challenge the term. They flag only places where it's aimed at someone the text itself shows was actually wronged (for example, a hard boundary crossed in Before Play).
3. **Some rewrites over-correct into both-sides hedging.** The guide warns that "repeating the disclaimer is fear wearing honesty's clothes." Where a proposed rewrite adds "maybe you did, maybe you didn't" to a passage that didn't need it, the better fix is usually to delete the verdict word and leave the sentence's force alone.

Side notes that are not pleading but turned up along the way: `all-power-is-mutual.md` links to Why Helping Is Hard for a claim ("hiding your self-interest makes you harder to trust") that the linked section doesn't make. Severity's "If you cry victim loud enough, you become the perpetrator" runs the other direction (a verdict on the accuser) and may deserve its own look.
